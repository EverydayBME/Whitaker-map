Fleur O’Hare

The people at the center of vision research

Melbourne vision research community

What does a senior clinical research coordinator do?

I help provide the framework for a research project, from the initial idea through to conducting the study and reporting the results. That can involve ethics applications, developing protocols with investigators, enrolling participants, and monitoring their safety and wellbeing.

I also have a clinical background, so I assess participants and carry out measurements. Depending on the project, I may work on data quality, analysis, papers, or presentations. There are many moving parts, but I enjoy being involved across the whole process.

What questions are you working on in vision research?

Much of my work involves people with inherited retinal diseases. We are investigating different approaches that might slow vision loss or help restore some vision, including visual prostheses, gene-therapy research, and neuroprotection strategies.

A substantial part of my work is a natural history study. People with different diagnoses come in for a range of assessments so we can learn which measurements best detect changes in vision and retinal health. That knowledge is important when designing future treatment trials.

Before testing whether something helps, we need to understand what change looks like and how best to measure it for a particular condition.

How did your clinical training lead you into research?

I trained in orthoptics. An honors project on outcomes after cataract surgery gave me my first experience of organizing and conducting research.

Afterward, I worked in clinical care, building my skills across different patients, ages, and conditions. Over time, my interest in improving patient care drew me back toward research and treatment trials.

One practical project involved medication labels. We were caring for people with visual impairment while expecting them to read very small print on eye drops and other treatments. Helping introduce large-print labels was a way to address a problem directly affecting daily care. Research can begin with something very practical that you notice while working with people.

I later worked in glaucoma research and then moved into retinal disease.

Why did you choose to work with eyes?

I studied art as well as science, and the idea of bringing them together appealed to me. There was something wonderful about looking at people’s eyes while working to improve their vision.

That interest in art has stayed with me. I enjoy screen printing, sculpture, and sewing, although friends and family have occasionally had enough of my homemade gifts. Making things is something I do for pleasure when I have time.

What makes this area rewarding?

Meeting people and hearing their stories is a large part of it. Some receive a diagnosis early in adult life, just as they are beginning a degree or career, and have to adapt to the possibility of losing vision.

Being involved at a time when researchers are developing potential treatments is meaningful. It can offer hope to people who were previously told there was nothing available for their condition.

That hope needs to be honest. There are only a limited number of approaches under investigation, not everyone will be suitable, and there is a great deal we do not yet know. But seeing knowledge develop during someone’s lifetime can matter enormously.

What do you gain from working with students and international collaborators?

Everyone can teach you something. Collaboration gives us a way to share knowledge and learn from people with different experience.

I enjoy working with students because the exchange goes both ways. We provide training and opportunities, but they contribute ideas and perspectives too. You never stop learning, regardless of your role.

How do you look after yourself outside work?

Exercise is very important to me. Making time for myself and getting to the gym helps me avoid becoming overwhelmed. I am interested in health and nutrition more broadly, but regular physical activity is especially valuable for my own wellbeing.

What is the next challenge for your studies?

We are broadening the natural history work to include some very rare retinal conditions. First, we need to find people with those diagnoses and learn more about their experiences and measurements.

That can be difficult. We contact eye-care practitioners, work through patient organizations and support groups, and use word of mouth and social media. Connecting with people is central to the research, both when a study begins and throughout their participation.

Anna Blakney & Robin Shattock

Engineering vaccines for a wider world

Imperial College London

Interview: 29 April 2019 · 4 minute read

Anna, what brought you to Imperial through the Whitaker program?

Anna: My Whitaker project involved lipid nanoparticle formulations for RNA vaccines, specifically in the context of an HIV vaccine. I knew people who had held international postdoctoral fellowships, and I knew Robin through collaborators from my PhD.

I applied with him and received the fellowship. Work from that period then helped me apply for a Marie Curie fellowship, jointly involving Molly Stevens, which provided another two years of funding here.

Robin, what does an engineer bring to your group?

Robin: Our work focuses on translating vaccine and antiviral ideas toward clinical use. We study HIV and emerging infections, among other areas.

Anna brought chemical engineering expertise into a group with a strong biological focus. That combination has been valuable for developing formulations and thinking about how concepts could move toward clinical testing. Different disciplines contribute different ways to approach the same problem.

What does the work look like day to day?

Anna: I spend much of my time in the laboratory. We generally begin by testing ideas in cells. If they look promising, we move into small-animal studies to investigate how a vaccine works and the mechanisms involved.

Robin: My role involves more of the broader planning: how to develop preventive strategies and technologies that could be faster, more affordable, and useful in different settings. I am interested in approaches that manufacturers in low- and middle-income countries could use, as well as those suited to large pharmaceutical facilities.

What is most exciting about the projects at this stage?

Anna: Some formulations developed during my fellowships are moving toward larger-animal studies, with the hope of progressing to human trials. Seeing something you worked on move along that path is exciting. Not every biomedical engineering project gets that close to a possible human application.

Robin: Alongside the RNA work, we are interested in modular manufacturing. Could vaccine production become much more compact and flexible, rather than always depending on a very large facility? That is an important question if we want manufacturing to be possible in more places.

How did you choose research as a career?

Anna: I did a lot of undergraduate research and found that I wanted to keep doing it. A PhD was the next step toward becoming an independent researcher, followed by postdoctoral work.

Robin: I would be careful about presenting any one career path as a formula. Mine has involved recognizing opportunities, adapting, and making the most of them. A fixed trajectory is not always the most useful way to think about a scientific career.

What would you tell someone early in their studies?

Robin: Look forward to the point when you can do your own experiments and develop your own ideas. Early study often involves learning existing knowledge and doing work with a fairly predictable outcome. Research requires something different: continually generating questions and ideas.

Anna: Stay open to opportunities and to people you meet. Looking back, my path was not a straight line. Networks and conversations helped create possibilities I could not have planned from the beginning.

Anna, what did living abroad add to the experience?

Anna: It takes you out of your comfort zone. You hear different attitudes and begin to think more clearly about what you value in science and in life.

There are practical adjustments too, from navigating a city to figuring out how to see a doctor. I had spent part of my PhD in South Africa, so I had already experienced a substantial international move. London involved another adjustment, but over time those unfamiliar routines become ordinary. For me, the overall experience has been positive.

How do you make room for life outside research?

Anna: I prefer starting early and leaving at a reasonable hour. I have found the culture around annual leave and time away in the United Kingdom helpful in maintaining that balance.

Robin: Time away matters for creativity. If you work continuously, you lose the space to think. Enjoying research is valuable, but that does not mean every hour should be spent doing it. Rest is part of remaining an inventive scientist.

What do you hope comes next?

Anna: I would like to keep working on innovative vaccines for global health and eventually lead my own group.

Robin: We hope to take vaccine approaches into clinical trials, learn how well they work, and develop the manufacturing platform further. For HIV, there are still fundamental questions about what vaccine design could provide protection. Clinical research is part of how we hope to answer them.

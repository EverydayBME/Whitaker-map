François Rannou

Bringing medicine, mechanics, and teaching together

Université Paris Descartes / Cochin Hospital

Interview: 27 May 2019 · 4 minute read

How do clinical medicine and research fit together in your work?

I am a professor of medicine, and my clinical work is in rehabilitation and rheumatology at Cochin Hospital. My research focuses on the molecular and cellular biology of joint diseases, including osteoarthritis.

Our group brings together people with medical, basic science, and engineering backgrounds. I want students to have opportunities to do fundamental research while remaining connected to questions that matter in medicine.

For me, the appeal is the combination of research, teaching, and clinical practice. They are different activities, and I enjoy that variety.

Did you know early that you wanted both medicine and science?

Yes. Both my parents were physicians, so medicine was familiar to me. But I was also interested in science and wanted to pursue the two together.

I completed scientific training alongside my medical education, including a master’s and PhD. I wanted to teach and carry out basic research as well as treat patients. That was a specific choice about the kind of career I hoped to have.

It is a long route, and it is not the route every physician needs to take. For me, the combination was what made it worthwhile.

What did your postdoctoral experience in California change?

I spent two years in California, from 2001 to 2003, and it was one of my best experiences. I was away from the system and colleagues I knew in Paris, working much more independently in a different environment.

Scientifically, I worked on mechanobiology: how mechanical forces can influence gene expression and cellular behavior. Much of that work involved vascular cells. I then brought those ideas to cartilage cells and questions about how mechanical stress affects their metabolism.

The experience and the work we published were important for my development. When I returned to France, I continued building a group around mechanobiology.

What do international visitors contribute to a laboratory?

The benefit goes both ways. A visiting student gains experience, but the host laboratory changes too.

For example, when an American student joined us, we held laboratory meetings in English. That required effort from the group, but it was valuable and had a lasting effect on how we communicated. It encouraged a more international environment.

An exchange is therefore more than a project for one student. It can influence the habits and openness of the whole group.

Why were you interested in developing an international biomedical engineering program?

We saw students who wanted to study biomedical engineering leaving France to find programs elsewhere. We wanted to develop an opportunity in Paris that connected engineering and medicine and welcomed people from different backgrounds.

Teaching the master’s program in English was an important part of that. It made it possible for students from many countries to participate and learn together.

Going abroad also demonstrates an ability to work in a different system. It can be difficult, but that experience is useful when students later pursue another degree or research position.

What helps students maintain a life outside demanding studies?

Sport is important. In some ways it has parallels with science: you train over time, work toward objectives, follow rules, and learn both individually and with other people.

Students should also make use of the life around them. In a city such as Paris, there are museums, cinemas, and many opportunities to meet people. Living in the city can be expensive and complicated, but it also offers a great deal beyond the university.

What makes your career meaningful to you?

Being part of a community and having room to pursue different kinds of work. I value caring for patients, asking research questions, and helping students learn.

It is not simply a financial choice. Academic medicine offers a particular kind of freedom and responsibility, and I find the combination stimulating. Doing only one activity every day would suit me less well.

Where do you see biomedical engineering contributing most?

The connection between engineers and physicians is going to be increasingly important. Developments in computing and other technologies are opening questions that medicine cannot address alone.

Molecular biology remains essential, but we also need close relationships with people who understand engineering methods and can help translate them into useful medical tools. Building those connections is one of the reasons I care about interdisciplinary training.

Gabriella Nilssonhall

Following tissue engineering across borders

KU Leuven

How did you come to KU Leuven?

I am from Sweden, where I did my bachelor’s degree and most of my master’s studies. During my master’s, I spent a year in France through Erasmus. I really enjoyed meeting people from other places who were studying similar things.

That experience encouraged me to go abroad again. After another year in Sweden, I looked for a thesis opportunity outside the country and found one in Belgium. I came here for the master’s thesis and then stayed for a PhD.

What first interested you in biomedical engineering?

At school, learning about DNA made a strong impression on me. I knew I wanted to do something involving medicine or human cells.

Later, a tissue engineering course during my master’s helped me find a more specific direction. The professor was inspiring because she connected the science to something that could eventually exist outside the classroom.

When you begin studying, there is a lot of theory, and it can be hard to see where it leads. That course helped me make the connection. You later discover how long the path toward an application can be, but seeing the possibility is what drew me in.

What does your workday involve?

It varies a great deal. Some days involve reading or writing at my desk; others involve cell culture, staining, and other laboratory work.

As I have moved toward the end of the PhD, helping other students has become a larger part of the job. I work with master’s thesis students and new PhD students, teaching methods and helping guide their projects.

I like that mixture. Research involves more than a single type of activity, and the balance changes as you gain experience.

What was different about moving to France and Belgium?

Language was the biggest initial challenge in France. I had studied French at school, but that was different from taking all my courses in French and using it every day. It took time before I felt comfortable having conversations.

The organizations supporting exchange students were enormously helpful, especially with paperwork and getting established. Without that support, I would have been much more lost.

In Belgium, language was easier because many people spoke English or French, but the bureaucracy was still something to learn. Being in an international group helped: there were people I could ask who had already gone through similar steps.

What motivates you most in research?

Seeing that the knowledge can lead somewhere. I want to know that what we learn is useful and can contribute to an application, even if that application is ten or twenty years away.

It does not have to happen immediately, but that connection matters to me. It is part of why tissue engineering caught my interest in the first place.

How do you manage time away from work?

It has had ups and downs. You can become so focused on a project that, after a while, you realize you are not doing much else.

I have had to make a conscious effort to switch off email on weekends and do other things. Having flexibility in the laboratory is valuable, but it also means that setting boundaries is partly your own responsibility.

Outside work, I enjoy traveling, discovering new places without too much planning, and spending a Sunday afternoon with a board game and a beer.

What decision comes after the PhD?

First, I need to defend it. Then there is the choice between continuing in academia and moving into industry.

A postdoc is one possible next step, but finding a longer-term academic position afterward is difficult. I am also thinking about where I could best help move research toward the clinic. That is a genuine decision for me, rather than something I have already settled.

How do you see the field developing?

I expect disciplines to become more closely connected, although learning to work together takes time. New techniques will also play a role, but we need to understand which applications really benefit from them.

Three-dimensional printing is a good example. It is an exciting tool, but using it does not automatically make a project better. The important question is whether it is the right method for the problem.

What would you tell a student considering international study?

Go and explore if you have the opportunity. You learn about yourself as well as your subject, and you meet people with different ways of working and living.

When you stay within a familiar setting, many social expectations are already understood. In an international environment, you have to learn more deliberately how to work with people. That experience has been valuable to me.

Why did you do a postdoc in BME at JHU instead of in Belgium?

So the reason why I did the post doc is because in Belgium you kind of need an experience abroad, and the post doc was I think an easy way to get that experience abroad. And it was also a way not to close any door in the sense that you know if you go into ... I feel that if you go into industry, it might be more difficult than to come back to academia, even though I know that people sometimes do that. We just hired someone in the school of engineering who is coming from 10 plus years in the industry. So I know it's possible, but I feel that it's more difficult. While I don't think that the post doc of one or two years is a big barrier to enter industry, I mean what's the difference between someone with a PhD or a PhD plus one or two years of post doc?

What is a way to get more into theory for BME development?

The thing is so you ask about how to get more into the theory. I think to do that, I mean you have to have a very good background to do that. I think even when I was in the lab, it's true that the research that you're doing is applied, but at the same time, I feel that when we discuss, you have these various theoretical backgrounds. And so I don't see that as a problem. I think you just have to find the right person to work with on the right project, and then I don't see why it would be difficult for you to transition, honestly.

Would you be able to tell us why you pursued a career in BME?

Yeah I think my whole family, there is a lot of medical doctors. So my grandfather was also a medical doctor. My grandmother was a nurse. On the other side of my family, I had a pharmacist. The sister of my father is also a medical doctor. So I have like a lot of that in my family, and at the same time, what I like with this field is that you can have a big impact on people life. And I think that's very rewarding. Even though what I've been doing so far is not directly applicable to patient health today, but I think if we want to be able to help patient, we need to also have like fundamental research that will one day maybe we'll be picked up by someone else and have a direct impact on someone. I think that knowing that is very motivating, at least for me. I'm also very interested in math and theoretical question, but knowing that you do what you do because maybe one day what you are doing today can be used to help someone, I think that's great.s

So you did a post doc at Johns Hopkins in Baltimore. Can you talk a little bit, how did this come about? I know you did a Fulbright. Can you expand a little bit about that and what that experience was like in terms of just transitioning from a European native to plopped into the United States?

Yeah so after my PhD, I really wanted to have some experience abroad. I wanted to really ... My first objective was to find a post doc in the US because I think that's where you need to go if you want to be exposed to premier universities, especially in the field of biomedical engineering. That was my main motivation, to be exposed to unique environment where I could learn a lot first, build great collaborations, and also maybe first build great collaborations and then also learn a lot about how things are done in the US and how we can maybe then now how I can use that knowledge to improve what is done in Belgium.

As I told you, when I started my studies in Liège there was no biomedical engineering degree. Now there is a biomedical engineering degree in Liège, but it's only 10 years old now. So it's quite young, and so I think a lot of the things that I saw at Hopkins, how the BME department is working are things that I can try to use here to improve what we offer as a program for students.

So after my PhD, I was not sure yet that I wanted to pursue an academic career. Because I was not sure, I think it would have not make sense to me to stay and do a post doc in Belgium or even in France because I think being in the US, at least have this experience of one or two years of full English life, and just also for that confidence and network. I mean also being exposed to a very different culture in terms of the working culture or even like the culture in general was really something valuable for me, and I knew that on my CV it would not be a disadvantage.

I know you're a Fulbright Scholar from Belgium. Can you comment a little about what that experience specifically was like? And how that helped in sort of achieving some of these goals.

I think the Fulbright Fellowship was I think very helpful in the sense that so I wanted to enter one of the top university, in Johns Hopkins university, doing BME in the world. Coming from the little university of Liège, I had like little power to enter like a big university, even though you have always connections between PI, but still I think what I wanted to do is to really present myself as an asset to the university where I would go.

And so in Belgium, you have essentially two very prestigious fellowship that allow you to leave Belgium and go for a post doc in the US. So you have what is called BAEF, so that's Belgo-American Foundation. But I missed the deadline for that one and so then you have the Fulbright. And so essentially what I did is that my PhD advisor met Dr. Sridevi Sarma at a conference on the workshop, pretty much at the same time where I was looking and trying to find a lab where I would have fun in the US. He thought it would be a good match so I started looking at what Sri was doing and indeed it was quite interesting so I essentially contacted Sri and said, "Okay I'd like to come and work in your lab, but what I can do is that we can apply together to this Fulbright Fellowship, and if I get it then I can come and you have to essentially pay me much less than a normal post doc." She was very interested by that, of course.

So then I had this interview process with Fulbright. I know that you have to submit a proposal and then you have a small panel that interview you and essentially ask standard interview questions. I think what they are mainly looking for is, of course, bright individual, but I think mostly what they are looking for is motivated people. I think I was able to show my motivation and my enthusiasm in first the research question that I was proposing to study in the US, and also in the project of going in the US in general.

Okay yeah so I guess last question I have is where ... Now that you're a PI, where do you sort of see the future of biomedical engineering going? Whenever you envision happening 10, maybe 20 years down the road?

I'm not sure I would speak for biomedical engineering in general. I would maybe feel more comfortable speaking about neural engineering because I think biomedical engineering is so broad. But I think definitely the future is the interaction between machines in general and humans. This interaction, I mean it could be like stimulator to help a patient. It could be prosthetics that I use to help education after some like injury, for example. And I think to be able to have this very good interface between machines and humans, there are still a lot of things that we need to understand.

We are now in a position to record the activity of thousand, hundreds of neurons at the same time. Same thing for the manipulation. We are very good at manipulating the activity of many neurons at the same time, but we are not able to understand the observations that we make. I think what is really critical is ... These manipulations, recordings that have improved for the past 10 years, I'm sure that in the next 10 years it will be like even more mind blowing what we can do. And I think that we don't have to ... I feel that sometimes when you talk to neural engineer, people working between engineering and neuroscience, about what they do, sometimes they tell you about the technique that they use. So a very popular technique right now is optogenetics. And so many times when you speak to people as a friend, for example, and you ask them what are you doing? They will tell you optogenetics. I mean that's not the research questions. Optogenetics is just a tool that you can use.

And so I think that sometimes people are blinded by the fact that we can from these great tools we can get a lot of data, but if we are not able to dissect that data and to ask very specific question that we can then address with these tools, then we won't move further. So yeah, a big thing that I think is important in terms of research question is really being able to explain the viability that we see in the data that we record. So you can have a human that is like if you think about model control, a human that is doing exactly the same movement again and again, but actually if you look at the microscopic level, the neurons that are activating for the same movements, at each movement they are different. The fibers that are activated in the muscle are different, and so being able to understand why we have this viability is something that will help us a lot moving forward.

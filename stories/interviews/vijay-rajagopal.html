Vijay Rajagopal

Finding the engineering inside a cell

University of Melbourne

What does your group want to understand?

I lead the Cell Structure Mechanobiology Group at the University of Melbourne. We want to understand how cells work using engineering, cell biology, applied mathematics, and computation.

A major interest is how heart cells grow and change in response to their environment. The heart can grow in circumstances such as athletic training or pregnancy, and growth also occurs in disease. We want to understand how individual cells sense those conditions and reorganize their internal structure.

We study calcium signaling, mechanics, and energy use, then try to connect them in models of cell function. Understanding those mechanisms could help us identify new approaches to heart disease.

How do you turn images of cells into models?

We bring together information from electron microscopy, super-resolution microscopy, and calcium imaging. Computer science methods help us extract information from those images and build three-dimensional representations.

The mathematical approach depends on the scale. We may model the opening and closing of individual ion channels probabilistically, then connect that to a description of behavior across the whole cell. The challenge is to connect the parts in a way that is both informative and computationally manageable.

For me, the images are an extraordinary source of questions. They show so much activity, and I want to understand the physical principles that make it work.

Were you always interested in cell biology?

No. I liked mathematics and physics, and I knew I wanted to do engineering. I had not studied biology at school. An engineering mathematics lecture changed things: seeing that mathematics could describe features of the human body really caught my attention.

My PhD at Auckland involved computational models of the breast for clinical imaging and decision-making. Toward the end, I came across David Goodsell’s book The Machinery of Life. I had not studied cell biology, and the idea of all that machinery operating inside cells amazed me.

I began wondering whether I could use my modeling techniques to understand that machinery. That curiosity helped move me from organ-scale mechanics into the work I do now.

How did you decide whether to pursue a PhD?

My father’s advice was to do one if the work I wanted required it. At the time, many of the biomedical engineering roles that interested me were research jobs for people with doctoral training, so that was part of my decision.

Initially, I imagined moving into a company afterward. But as my interests changed, I realized I wanted room to explore questions that seemed best suited to academia. It was not a carefully calculated career strategy. I kept following work that I wanted to do.

What have you learned from mentors?

Different people have helped in different ways. Peter Hunter showed me the importance of a broad vision and of building an environment where people feel they are contributing to something larger.

Martyn Nash’s practical support for students meant a great deal to me. Paul Nielsen encouraged thinking beyond the obvious approach. Other mentors have helped me think more ambitiously about research or approach a problem with greater precision.

Mentoring does not always need a formal label. Some of my collaborators are also people I turn to for advice.

Why are international collaborations important to your work?

I look for people with the expertise a problem needs. They are not always nearby, so international collaboration is part of doing the research well.

For a specialized field, it would be limiting to look only within your own institution or country. The important boundaries to cross are often disciplinary ones. I want to work with people whose skills complement mine and learn from how they approach questions.

Maintaining those relationships takes effort, particularly from Australia and New Zealand. Travel can be valuable, although family responsibilities also affect what is possible.

How do you navigate family life and career expectations?

I am still figuring it out. There is a real push and pull, and I do not think it helps to pretend that everything is straightforward.

Changing fields and making time for family can affect the pace of a career. It can be difficult to compare yourself with people who finished their PhDs at the same time. But not reaching a particular position now does not mean you will never reach it.

Taking a longer view helps. So does having serious conversations with your partner about what matters and how you will manage it. When people talk openly, you realize that very few careers follow the simple ladder that the reward systems seem to suggest.

What would you like people to appreciate about modeling biology?

No single model will explain everything in a cell. That does not mean we should avoid modeling. We can begin with a defined component or a simplified system and learn something about the principles involved.

Those principles may then help us understand another system or design something new. I want there to be space for that curiosity-driven work: identifying a gap in knowledge and investigating it, even when the immediate application is not yet clear.

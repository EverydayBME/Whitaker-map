Marloes Groot

Taking a microscope into the operating room

Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam (VU)

What is the goal of your imaging research?

We use higher harmonic generation microscopy to look into tissue. Some of the work is curiosity-driven, studying biological processes, and some is aimed at clinical questions.

A major goal is rapid tissue assessment. We have studied brain tumor tissue and investigated whether the images show features that surgeons and pathologists need to see. We are also working toward putting the imaging capability into a needle, so that it might eventually be used directly within tissue.

How close is the technology to the hospital?

We started a company that has turned a large laboratory setup into something compact and transportable on a cart. At the time of this interview, we are completing the permissions and preparation needed to bring it into the hospital.

Although much of our earlier data came from brain tissue, our initial hospital work is with lung surgery collaborators. We also work with people interested in burns and scar tissue.

Moving from the laboratory to that setting is a significant step. It means thinking about the instrument as something other people need to use, not only as a research setup.

How did a physicist studying photosynthesis arrive at medical imaging?

My PhD and postdoctoral work focused on photosynthesis and protein dynamics, using lasers and spectroscopy. Later, I began thinking about a new direction.

Neurobiologists asked whether we could make something that would let them look deeper into tissue. Through them, I met neurosurgeons and learned about questions they wanted imaging to answer during surgery.

That combination mattered: I was ready to explore something different, and collaborators had a problem for which our methods might be useful.

Did anyone help change how you approached the project?

Yes. I was focused on developing the needle-based approach, but a postdoc suggested starting with a simpler microscope for analyzing removed tissue.

That was an important practical idea. It gave us a way to develop the technique and learn what it could show while continuing to work toward the more ambitious in-tissue application.

What first made you want to do research?

As a physics student, I enjoyed the point when we moved from taking courses into doing experiments. Being the first person to make a measurement, and then having the responsibility to interpret it, was very stimulating.

My supervisor encouraged me to continue into a postdoc abroad. I had trained in a successful group with strong facilities, and I wanted to know whether I could do well in a different environment too.

Working in Chicago and France helped answer that question. It also showed me different ways of organizing research and scientific life.

What did you notice about those different environments?

In Chicago, I was surprised to discover how many colleagues worked at weekends. In the Netherlands, taking weekends off had been more normal for me. There were also differences in hierarchy and in how social life centered on the university.

In France, the research environment involved more permanent staff and fewer graduate students and postdocs. The science was very strong, but the social experience was different again.

Those experiences were valuable because they showed that a laboratory culture is not the only possible way to work.

How do you organize life around a demanding job?

I try to work efficiently while I am here and make room for other parts of life. There are times when an experiment requires a long stretch, but that does not mean every weekend should disappear into work.

Having children also creates a clear reason to stop and go home. I enjoy reading, exercise, and horse riding, which is something my children do as well.

What motivates you about the clinical direction?

It is powerful to know that someone is waiting for the thing you are developing. When a surgeon calls because an imaging tool could help answer a question from an operation, the need becomes very concrete.

I enjoyed fundamental research too. But this gives the same scientific methods another kind of purpose, and I find that very motivating.

What advice would you give someone choosing a research group?

Look for an environment with appropriate supervision, a realistic project, and the resources to do it well. A very difficult project without enough support can make it hard to demonstrate what you are capable of.

Expose yourself to new challenges and see how you respond. Science offers room to change direction, as I did. You do not need to know every future step before beginning.

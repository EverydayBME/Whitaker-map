Ton van der Steen

Listening for better ways to see the heart

Erasmus MC (Erasmus University Medical Center)

What does your group work on at Erasmus MC?

We develop technical approaches for cardiovascular medicine, especially diagnosis and guidance of treatment. Much of the work uses ultrasound, alongside optical methods and combinations of the two.

The group includes researchers working on different parts of that problem. My role involves supporting them and building connections—with clinicians, companies, and funding organizations—so that the engineering and the medical questions stay connected.

Can you give an example of the technology?

One area is high-frame-rate ultrasound imaging. We are developing ways to use it to study heart function and blood flow. Another area is catheter-based imaging to help guide interventions inside blood vessels.

We also work with ultrasound contrast agents: very small gas bubbles that travel in the bloodstream. Special ultrasound sequences allow us to detect them and study where blood is reaching tissue.

That information can help researchers investigate perfusion and vascular structure. We are interested both in improving the images and in understanding how they could be useful for a particular clinical question.

How did you find your way into medical ultrasound?

I studied applied physics at Delft and was always interested in sound, hearing, music, and acoustics. During my studies, I decided I wanted to apply that interest to something medical.

I visited several academic hospitals while looking for a master’s project. When I came to this laboratory, I liked the atmosphere: high-quality work, people who were enthusiastic, and a practical, relaxed approach.

I did my master’s work here, went to Nijmegen for a PhD in medical sciences, and then returned. A visit to a laboratory helped shape where I would spend much of my career.

What do you look for in an international collaboration?

The expertise needed to answer the question. If we understand one part of a problem and need help with another, we look for people who are particularly good at that part, wherever they are.

They might be nearby or on another continent. The group itself is international, and we work with colleagues in several countries. Geography is less important than finding a good scientific fit and a productive relationship.

Over time, many of those colleagues become friends. That makes travel feel less like repeatedly arriving among strangers and more like joining people you know.

How did you become involved with the Whitaker program?

I learned about the foundation through professional connections and later heard about the fellowships. I contacted colleagues in the United States to ask whether they knew students who might be interested.

That led to fellows coming from Duke and Cincinnati. The program offered an opportunity for students to work in a different environment with considerable freedom to explore.

What would you say to a student who worries they are too inexperienced to contribute?

Look for an environment that inspires you, with people whose work you want to learn from. Then approach them and discuss what you could do together.

An open-ended opportunity can feel intimidating, especially compared with a project whose boundaries are already defined. But being early in your career does not mean you have nothing to offer. Interest, initiative, and a willingness to learn matter.

What did you learn from your own mentors?

I valued a mentor who could bring out the best in people without constantly pressing them. He gave people room to pursue what they believed was important while helping them understand the context in which they worked.

That included maintaining strong relationships with the clinical colleagues around us. If you develop a new technique, the people nearby who share the work and its responsibilities are important partners.

I try to provide a similar combination of freedom and guidance.

Where do you see biomedical engineering making a broader difference?

Helping people remain active and independent as they get older. That could involve better detection of disease, less invasive treatment, or technologies that support people in daily life.

Imaging can help us understand a problem earlier or guide treatment more precisely. Less traumatic interventions may help people recover and return to normal activities sooner. Those are important parts of making healthcare more sustainable.

What do you enjoy outside work?

Music and travel. I have played music, especially African music, and enjoy listening to live performances. If a conference takes me somewhere with a strong musical culture, I like to explore it.

I also played volleyball for a long time. Interests outside work are valuable for their own sake and because they connect you with people who have nothing to do with your research.

What advice would you give your younger self?

Pay attention to what you actually want to do, and set boundaries around it. Finding work you care about gives you a reason to develop your abilities, but it should still be part of a life that includes other people and interests.

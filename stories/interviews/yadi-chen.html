Yadi Chen, PhD Candidate, Department of Physiology, Faculty of Medical and Health Sciences, University of Auckland, Auckland, New Zealand

May you please introduce yourself?

My name is Yadi Chen, and I am almost finished with my third year of my PhD in Physiology.

What is something that you're especially excited about in your research?

One thing that I'm most excited about is my work relating the lens cellular physiology to the lens optics. This has never been done by any other people. Even though our lab is mainly focused on the lens’ cellular physiology, all of those [physiologic] changes can actually relate to, or even truly lead to the disorder of the lens function in terms of the transparency or the ability to focus on far away things or close by things. Our lab, and then myself, mainly focus on what is the underlying mechanism that has caused those age-related eye diseases. Using the system that I have learned, developed, and set up, I can actually investigate what might be the possibilities that lead to those kind of eye diseases. Maybe even, based on my work, some other new treatments for those diseases can be developed.

That is very cool! Did you start your undergraduate education in Bioengineering?

Yes, I did. I completed my high school in China and then I came to New Zealand where I did my foundation courses. This is a one-year foundation course where they teach math, physics, and other things. During that year, I was thinking, what should I do for university? Originally, I was sure that I wanted to do engineering, but I was not sure about which- civil or chemical or BME. Then, an older friend of mine who did the BME courses was like, "you should book those, and this is actually really so fun!” To be honest, I didn't think too much, so I just did the course following her suggestion!

What did you find most appealing about the field of BME?

I think it is quite different to what other people are doing. You have the chance to do your own project and then to do your own things that are all related to medical. I believe that medicine is a very interesting area that almost everyone can get into it. By binding engineering with the medical aspects, it is a very unique way to learn about the basics of engineering and also to get into a part of the medical field.

What does your day to day work look like?

It depends. In here you have to plan experiments and events. For this week, I may just do my experiments for the whole break. Then, the following week I will do the data processes. This is the current situation because I have the system being set up, but during the first year and second year of my PhD, I was still booting up the system that I'm using right now. During those times I was in the lab the whole day trying to get the thing working. Now I am more focused on the medical part, like the fidelity part by using the system that I built.

Where would you like your see your career go?

That's an interesting question because us PhD students, you either do academic or go to industry, and it will depend on your interests. Then, you may find yourself eventually circling them around. For myself, I am currently at the end of my PhD. I am thinking about staying academic, but I do want to get some experience or feeling about the industry part of working life. In terms of my career, I am trying to do as much as I can to finish my PhD. Then, I might try to spend half a year to go to industry and to get some experience. If I like it I may stay there, but if not then I may come back and do some other researches.

What are the most exciting questions being answered in your field today? About lens physiology, for example?

There are so many things! I mean, if you just think about the lens, it is just a transparent structure. It does not really have any blood supply. So why it is transparent? How can it keep the transparent property, mostly through our whole life? Even though this is still an unclear question, a lot of the basic fundamental answers has been provided. One of which is the microcirculation system that can actively brings nutrients into the lens center and then remove the waste product and water from the core. This is a way to keep this lens transparent because, if it doesn't really have any blood supply, we need a way to maintain the lens homeostasis. Apparently one answer to this question [about transparency] is the microcirculation system.

Elyse Watkins

ETH Zurich

18 October 2019 · 2 minute read

In this conversation

“I really liked asking questions or trying to solve problems for which we don't have any answers. And so I decided that the only thing that could get me to trying to solve those problems and ask those questions is a PhD.”

“Some people are still against having so many authors [on articles]. I think that's foolish. If someone contributed to the work, the more the merrier. As a PhD, you don't need to learn every technique that there is. I think the field would move much more quickly if we would get people with different skills to work on a problem. You'll use your expertise and collaborate with others to use theirs. And the whole project moves much more rapidly. So I think being okay with having a lot of authors and having different expertise come onto a project, I think that's great.”

What frustrates you now?

I have done some outreach events where I speak about careers in STEM to elementary and middle school students. What I’ve noticed that frustrates me is that up until maybe 6th grade, boys and girls raise their hands equally, even perhaps more the girls, in trying to answer questions with regards to STEM. For some reason, when we call on the seventh and eighth graders, the girls stop asking questions. I don’t know why that is. And I see some of that carry over into academia. From my experience, it seems that men are more willing to speak up at conferences, and I hope that changes.

Looking back at your research experience abroad, what stands out?

So something that I love about the Europeans, and I don't know if this was just this lab specifically, is that they're so tight knit in their activities that they do together. They do incredible science and work really hard, but then also put a lot of value on their interpersonal relationships and life outside of the lab. They also get coffee together at least three times a day in the lab. First thing in the morning, right after lunch, and maybe a couple of times after that. During their breaks they would talk about science, but also about life. Which I was very ok with, because I love coffee and I love talking.

Oh, just I think one of my favorite moments was how much they took me in the lab when I first arrived. The first week I was there a group of labmates invited me to bike over to France to go to a farmer’s market! One coworker said I could use her bike for the month (although I quickly became traumatized by biking in Basel when my tire got stuck in tram tracks and I faceplanted in the middle of the road). They invited me to ice cream, to hang out by the river, to get coffee. They freely told me about all of their projects, and were receptive to my offering to help with some techniques. On my last evening, after me having been there for only a month, they planned a BBQ by the river and we hung out all night. It was a really special time.

They'd bring the little portable barbecue?

Yes. And everyone brings their own meat. That's super cute.

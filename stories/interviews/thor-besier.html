Thor Besier

Understanding movement beyond the laboratory

Auckland Bioengineering Institute, University of Auckland

How would you describe the central question in your work?

I call myself a biomechanist. I am interested in the mechanical mechanisms of injury and disease: how movement and the forces inside the body affect tissues and joints.

We build computational models based on physics and physiology, then bring in information from medical images, movement measurements, and wearable sensors. The aim is to understand the relationship between structure and function and apply that understanding to clinical problems.

You might observe how someone walks, but that does not directly tell you the forces acting inside their knee or how those forces are distributed among tissues. Models help us make that connection. It matters for joint replacement, rehabilitation, and even tissue engineering, where you need to know the mechanical environment a replacement tissue will encounter.

How can that help someone recovering from a stroke?

A stroke can change muscle activation and movement patterns. We want to understand those changes and investigate ways to improve movement.

One approach is real-time feedback. We measure what a person is doing and provide a signal, such as a small vibration on the body, to help them change a movement pattern. We have explored related approaches in other conditions, including knee problems.

The tools can be shared across projects, but the question remains specific to the person and the condition: what would a useful change in movement look like, and how could we support it?

Why is measuring movement outside the lab so important?

A laboratory or clinic gives us a limited view. After a joint replacement, for example, a clinician may see a patient for rehabilitation but know very little about how they move for the rest of the week. Wearable sensors offer a way to study that daily activity.

Context also changes movement. In earlier work on sporting maneuvers, we asked people to respond to a light that told them which direction to move at the last moment. Their joint loads were very different from those in a planned maneuver, even though the basic task was the same.

That is part of what makes real-world measurement exciting. We need to understand movement as it happens, with reactions, fatigue, and changing surroundings, rather than only in a carefully controlled task.

How do you know whether a computational model is giving a useful answer?

We have to test its predictions against experiments. In one collaborative project, groups receive the same experimental data and build their own models using different tools and workflows.

The experimental work uses cadaveric joints and robotic rigs to measure quantities such as forces and pressures. We then ask whether the different modeling approaches arrive at the same answers and, if they do not, where they diverge.

That process matters if models are going to inform decisions about implants or other medical devices. A detailed model is only useful if we understand what its predictions can support.

What brought you back to New Zealand after working abroad?

I spent seven years in Western Australia and eight at Stanford. Those experiences gave me research skills and lasting collaborations. I had originally applied for a fellowship that would have brought me to Auckland earlier, but while I was on its waiting list, an opportunity at Stanford came through a colleague I had met at a conference.

Later, after we had children, returning to New Zealand became more appealing. I wanted to be closer to family. I contacted Peter Hunter about possibilities, and an opportunity came together quite quickly.

The international relationships did not stop when I moved. They came back with me and continued to support collaborations and student exchanges.

What do people with different training bring to your group?

Different strengths. I had to learn programming and engineering methods along the way, whereas many students arrive with those skills already. My background gave me anatomy and physiology experience that I can share with them.

There is a big difference between a clean diagram of a knee and seeing an actual knee. Taking engineering students into the anatomy laboratory helps make that connection. They teach me things too.

That variety is one of the strengths of the institute. People arrive by different routes, and the musculoskeletal group works across those boundaries through shared projects and co-supervision.

How do you approach mentoring and work–life balance?

A PhD should be a path toward independence. We meet to discuss research and problems, but students also need to learn to manage their time. I care about getting the work done, rather than measuring commitment by hours spent in the laboratory.

There is always more research to do. When we had children, I decided I did not want family life to be separate from everything else. I arranged time with them during the week and have no regrets about that.

Work is still busy, and sometimes I catch up in the evening. But you need time for yourself and your family. The work will not simply run out if you keep going longer.

What do you see as the next big challenge in biomechanics?

We need to bring the nervous system back into our models. Biomechanics often focuses on tissues and mechanical loads, but movement is controlled by the brain and informed by sensory feedback.

Combining biomechanics, neuroscience, and motor control could help us understand how people learn and adapt their movement. Alongside better real-world measurements and better-validated models, that is a direction I find very exciting.

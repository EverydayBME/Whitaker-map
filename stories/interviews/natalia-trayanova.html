Natalia Trayanova

Learning to see the bigger scientific picture

Johns Hopkins University

What first connected your interest in physics with biology?

I liked physics very much. While I was studying it, my father brought home a book on bioelectric phenomena by Robert Plonsey. It was the first time I really saw how physics could be applied to biology, and I loved that connection.

Later, when I had an opportunity to come to the United States through a National Academy of Sciences fellowship, I chose Duke because of that work. My route into the United States was unusual and shaped by circumstances very different from those of a student freely choosing among international programs. But choosing Duke was a decision I could make, and the science drew me there.

How did your early academic career develop?

I first came on the fellowship, returned to Bulgaria to defend my PhD, and then came back to Duke for postdoctoral work. I worked in an engineering research center that brought together clinical and biomedical engineering interests.

Later, as a research assistant professor, I received Whitaker support and taught bioelectricity. Eventually, an opportunity at Tulane led me to move there as an associate professor.

Looking back, part of wanting to stay at Duke was being afraid to move. Taking the new position meant moving beyond the setting I knew.

What did you have to learn about establishing yourself?

A great deal beyond doing the research itself. I did not feel I had an established network or a mentor showing me how to navigate the system. I had to learn how to communicate the work, write grants, and make a case for an idea.

Rejection played an important role. I read the comments, looked for examples of successful proposals, and tried to understand what made them effective. Serving on a review panel was especially useful because I could see how clearly some people presented their ideas.

That is when I became very aware of the power of words. The research matters, but so does whether another person can understand why it matters.

What did you value about the environment at Tulane?

It was a small department that cared about its students and the quality of teaching. I taught more there than I do now, and the smaller classes gave me a chance to know students individually.

The research group also grew. We developed whole-heart modeling work and had strong students and postdocs. From early on, I wanted the people in my laboratory to be visible and to do well in their own careers.

At that stage, I was often focused on the next scientific step. The broader view of where the work might go developed later.

What ultimately brought you to Johns Hopkins?

After Hurricane Katrina displaced our laboratory, colleagues in St. Louis provided space for us to work. Being away from New Orleans also made me reconsider a future that I had assumed would remain there.

I began receiving invitations and offers from other institutions and spent considerable time considering the possibilities. The decisive factor for Hopkins was the potential for clinical collaboration.

There was an established culture of biomedical engineers and clinicians working together. I wanted that environment for the research I hoped to do. The city itself mattered too; I felt a connection with Baltimore.

How did moving toward clinical questions change your thinking?

In basic science, you can focus on a tightly defined mechanism and very precise measurements. Clinical problems are more complicated and less controlled.

You have to ask which details matter for the question you are trying to answer. Is the work helping us understand something meaningful for human health? That does not mean details are unimportant; it means understanding when a detail changes the conclusion and when it does not.

I have become more interested in the larger picture and in pursuing different questions within the field, rather than defining my entire career around one narrow subject.

Was developing that broader view a deliberate effort?

Yes. Preparing a Pioneer proposal pushed me to think about the larger opportunity: what could be transformative, where the work could go, and how to make that direction understandable to other people.

It required stepping back from the immediate experiment or model. I learned to look for the central idea and explain why it could open something important.

I made a similar decision about leadership. I did not want to become a dean or department chair. I wanted the structure and shared commitment of a research center to support the scientific direction I had in mind.

What has helped you persist through setbacks?

A strong belief in the work and a willingness to keep trying after people say no. I have faced many rejections, but I have not treated them as the end of the effort.

Ambition has been a major driving force for me. It can also create pressure. There is no simple reward waiting at the end of every challenge; much of that drive comes from within.

What is difficult at this stage of your career?

Protecting time for the science itself. There are many small tasks with deadlines, and those can crowd out the reading and thinking I most want to do.

That can leave me feeling behind even when the immediate administrative work is getting done. Having the right support and infrastructure matters because a larger scientific ambition still needs time and attention.

How do you make room for enjoyment outside work?

I work a great deal, and I would like to be better at balancing it. But I do make room for people, cultural activities, and travel. Even adding a day to a conference trip can give me a chance to enjoy a place.

Those experiences help keep me going. For me, a meaningful career ultimately involves work that makes me happy and gives me room to pursue interesting ideas.

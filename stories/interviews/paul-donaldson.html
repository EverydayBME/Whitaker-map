Paul Donaldson

Understanding the living lens

University of Auckland

What makes the eye’s lens an interesting research problem?

The lens is a collection of living cells that must remain transparent and focus light. A pane of glass is transparent, but the lens also needs the right shape and optical properties throughout life.

It has no blood vessels. Instead, ion transport drives an internal circulation of water that helps deliver nutrients and remove waste. We want to understand how that system maintains the lens, and how changes in it may contribute to problems at different ages.

My background is in physiology and epithelial transport, so I came to the lens by asking how it functions. Some of its proteins have been there since before birth. Understanding how they are maintained over a lifetime is a remarkable problem.

How did bioengineering become part of that work?

I became interested in how the Auckland Bioengineering Institute used computer models to understand complex organs. The lens seemed a good example of a system in which cellular processes, tissue structure, and overall function are closely connected.

We began combining physiological models with measurements of shape and optical properties. MRI can provide information about lens shape and the water-to-protein ratio, which helps us model its refractive properties. We could then ask how changing the physiology might change the image formed by the lens.

We also needed practical ways to measure those changes. That led us toward laser ray tracing, where we follow how light travels through the lens and use those paths to infer its optical properties.

How did a Whitaker fellowship contribute to the collaboration?

I met Bianca at a conference in Hawaii. She was working with a group in Miami interested in lens accommodation, the process of changing focus. Their work brought expertise in biomechanics, optics, and devices for studying how lenses stretch. We brought knowledge of lens physiology.

We talked about the possibility of her coming to New Zealand, and the Whitaker fellowship made it possible. She spent two years with us and helped develop the laser ray-tracing system alongside a PhD student. That combined her engineering skills with an opportunity to learn more biology and gain some supervision experience.

The relationship continued after she returned to Miami. That is one of the valuable things about an exchange: it can create a collaboration that lasts much longer than the visit itself.

What does the ray-tracing system allow you to investigate?

We can change aspects of the lens’s physiology and measure what happens to its optics. MRI is valuable, but it is expensive and relatively slow for repeated measurements. The ray-tracing approach gives us another way to follow changes over time.

Initially, we were good at disrupting the circulation system and observing what went wrong. The next question is whether we can stimulate or modulate it in useful ways. Could changing transport processes help preserve optical function or reduce stiffness? Those are research questions, rather than established treatments.

Why are you interested in pressure inside the lens?

Water entering the lens has to leave again. Models predicted that its movement through the cell-to-cell pathway would generate an internal pressure, and experiments subsequently measured that pressure.

Think of water moving through a hose: changing resistance changes pressure. In the lens, there also appears to be regulation that keeps pressure around a set point. We are investigating whether altering that regulation changes the lens’s optical properties.

One of our projects has the title “Spectacles in a Bottle.” The idea is to explore whether pharmacology could alter the optics of the lens. I do not know whether it will work, but it is a good title. The important point is that we are looking at the lens as an active physiological system.

Was your career planned from the start?

It was more a series of opportunities. At university I tried both law and science, then chose science. I was interested in marine biology, but the reality of cold mudflats made physiology more appealing.

A visiting researcher encouraged me to do a postdoc in the United States. I went to Yale, and the laboratory then moved to the University of Texas Medical Branch in Galveston. I always wanted to return to New Zealand.

When I visited Auckland, I met a researcher working on the lens who wanted a physiologist to help understand protein function. There was no position waiting, but we wrote a grant together and made the move possible. That encounter shaped the rest of my career.

How have leadership roles affected your science?

Leading optometry exposed me to different questions about optics and focusing. Later, leading the School of Medical Sciences allowed me to combine science with broader leadership, while people in my laboratory took on more responsibility and developed their own areas.

Our laboratory brings together optics, physiology, molecular biology, and other expertise. We start with the problem—how the lens works—and find the techniques we need to study it.

What do you find most rewarding?

The people. Seeing someone arrive as a summer student, complete a PhD, work abroad, and return as a colleague is enormously satisfying. Over time, you get to know their families as well as their science.

In New Zealand we talk about whānau, an extended family. That is how the research community can feel. Conferences and exchanges create those relationships, and shared scientific questions keep them going.

How do you make room for life outside work?

I am not sure work and life are ever perfectly balanced. A grant deadline has to be met, but a child’s birthday matters too. I have tried to be home for family meals and make time for sport and recitals.

University work offers flexibility, although sometimes that means working at other times to compensate. The important thing is to make the most of family time when you have it.

N’Dea Irvin-Choy

University of Delaware

22 September 2020 · 11 minute read

In this conversation
  • May you please introduce yourself?
  • What led you to pursue an advanced degree in biomedical engineering?
  • I cannot imagine balancing those. How did you do it?

May you please introduce yourself?

My name is N'Dea Irvin-Choy, and I'm on Instagram and on Twitter as @bmekween. I am a third-year student at the University of Delaware. I am co-advised by two different advisors in the BME department, and the project that I work on is to design nanoparticles that can travel safely to the reproductive organs during pregnancy to target specific pregnancy-related complications. There is a wide variety that can be targeted, but one that I'm specifically looking at is bacterial vaginosis, which contributes to miscarriages in women that have it.

What led you to pursue an advanced degree in biomedical engineering?

It's funny, I actually never wanted to be a scientist. I wanted to have my own makeup line. When I was in high school, the people that were really doing that were all the basketball wives, the baseball wives, the football wives. I felt like this was clearly the industry I needed to get into in order to start my own brand, right? This was before social media and just when reality TV was huge.

Well, I was good at math, I was good at science, but I didn't have an interest in a science career. In high school, I had this really awesome AP chemistry teacher. She was the person who really taught me how to think critically and how to solve problems. I think that was my first real exposure to engineering, even though it was just a chemistry course. Because I was so into makeup, I was telling everyone, “I knew, I want to do makeup. That's what I want to do. That's where I want to go.” My dad was like, “Chemical engineers actually formulate makeup. They're the people who come up with the different formulas of how much of this chemical to use, how much of this glitter to use, and how much pink and purple and all that stuff to mix together so that you have really great makeup.” I was like, “Well, that sounds great!” It was actually funny because most dads don't like when their daughters get into that phase. They're like, "I look amazing. I want to wear this and do that." My dad was like, "You should make a career out of this." From that point on, I was like, “Okay, I'm going into chemical engineering. I'm good at math, I'm good at chemistry. This is great.” However, I was also playing field hockey in high school and I wanted to play in college. I was looking for different programs that would allow me to do both engineering and sports, and there are not a lot that do that because engineering is so intense and sports are also so intense. The school that I really wanted to go to is the school I ended going to – Rowan University – and I was being recruited by the coach there.

And I was like, "I really want to get into chemical engineering." And she was like, "We don't have anyone that's in that major. It's really hard to get into. I don't really know." And I was like, "Well, just put in my application and we'll see where it goes." And I actually didn't get in. So she was like, "What we can do is, I can resubmit your application and you can do our," it's called, "exploratory studies," but it was the undecided major at Rowan. And she was like, "Just do that for a year. You'll take all the same classes as all the engineers and then you'll reapply for the next year." So I had to wait a full year until I could apply again for that.

Oh, my God, it was awful. So the thing was, I was good at field hockey at the time. I was good at all the gen ed classes. It was calculus. Everything that I had in high school, I had again in college. So I was like, "This is a breeze," whatever. So the requirements for our engineering programs was you had to maintain at least a 3.0 to stay in if you were already in after your first year. So if you wanted to get in, you just had to apply and they will look at your transcripts and stuff and decide whether or not you got in.

So I think at the end of the year I had 3.6 or a 3.5, something like that. So it was pretty high. It was higher than most people in my classes that were engineers. And I was confident. I'm like this is going to be great. I'm going to get in. And I didn't get in. And I was like, all right. And I went back to my coach and I'm like, listen, I can't stay here and play field hockey if I don't get into the engineering program.

Yeah. So she was a huge help. And all my coaches have really been super incremental to my success. A lot of people write off sports, but it's really, really helped me. So she talked to one of our ... We have an athletic director and we have an athletic advisor. So she talked to both of them and they were like, "We're going to talk to the chairperson of the engineering school and see what we can do." So I got an email a couple of days later from our athletic advisor. And he was like, "Okay, chemical engineering is just not budging, but we have this program that's brand new. No one else has ever done it. It's called biomedical engineering. I don't know if you'll be interested in it, but they're going to let you in. So just feel it out. Let me know if you want to be transferred into this program."

So naturally, I went to Google and I'm like, "What the heck is biomedical engineering?" And I looked for it through all these different job descriptions and almost everything said people that make prosthetics, people that fix equipment in hospitals, or people that make dentures and stuff like that. And I was like, all right, well, it has engineering and the title, I'll just go with it. So I didn't know a single thing about it, but I just joined. And I was like, all right, I'm going to try it, and now I'm an engineer. So when I started the program, now I was in all of the ... I should mention, the same level as everyone else. So I had the same courses for my first year. And my second year was the first year of all your engineering courses. And I hated it. It was awful. It was the worst experience. I actually hated it.

This sucks. So because I was still playing field hockey, I had to take certain courses. And those, the sections that were available were all honor sections. So people that I didn't know that were doing higher-level work than I was used to doing, and I was still balancing field hockey as well. So I was like this is just way too much for me to handle. I really didn't get along with anyone in my groups or any projects that I had to do. I was behind all the time. I had to leave practice to go to office hours and stuff.

I cannot imagine balancing those. How did you do it?

Oh, gosh, I have no idea. So things I guess in academic-wise weren't well, but also field hockey wasn't going well because we've got a new coach. So when I was promised a spot the year before, I was kicked out of my spot and I was like, oh, my gosh, the world is shattering around me basically. And it got to a point where I was like I need to either drop field hockey or drop my major and make a game-time decision, essentially. So what actually ended up happening was that I started to get ... This is what kept me going. I started to get playtime field hockey. I was getting more play in high-profile games, with really tough opponents and stuff.

And I was like, okay, if this is looking better than maybe I can just stick it out this semester, finish a semester and then figure out what major to switch into because I don't want the one that I'm in. So by the end of that semester, I'd actually met other girls that were in my major that were also athletes. So they didn't play field hockey, but there was a swimmer, a lacrosse girl, and a soccer.

Yes. Yes. So they became my group of friends. And we would make fun of ourselves. We were all carrying our practice clothes to class. And then we'd all have to leave, or bring each other food, or Gatorade, and different stuff like that.

They were there. I still talk with them now. I'm going to one of their weddings next year. So I'm really excited. But so I made friends with them and I was like, okay, well, now that I have friends, maybe I'll just see how the second half of sophomore year goes and see what happens. It turned out to be, well, it wasn't great, but I didn't struggle as much as I had in the previous semester. So I was like, oh, this isn't that bad. But I still didn't really have a real interest in the major. I was doing well in classes. I had friends and I really liked playing field hockey, but I didn't know what I was going to do or where I was going to go with this major. Just kind of have the title. I was really just going with the flow at this point still.

So when I was a junior, we started to have more classes that were more geared towards real-life applications. And then we had another class that was, we call it a clinic, but it's basically a senior design course where you work on a project.

Yeah. I started to do this orthopedic project. And my advisor for the project was actually a retired orthopedic surgeon. So he had just left surgery and he was starting to teach at one of our, we had an affiliated med school, so he was starting to teach at the med school. And he was like, I really want to do research and work with the engineers and stuff like that because he didn't have an engineering background. So when I started working with him on the project, it was a whole nother ball game because he spoke this medical jargon language and I spoke engineering, and there weren't a lot of people ... Everyone in my group kind of had any experience with either coding or software design or something that was technical, and I had absolutely no experience besides playing field hockey. That was really the only thing I did other than go to class.

So there was a really long time, a learning curve really, for me to, one, understand what he was asking me for, and then to be able to communicate with him effectively, and then learn the software and learn how to do everything. Everything that was detail-oriented, I really, it took me a very long time to catch on. But the longer I worked with him, the more ... I just had to put in a lot of time outside of that class. But once I started to learn the actual software and how to use it, and how to design things, it was a piece of cake. We got along so well. And he began to write me letters of rec for internships and stuff.

So I was like, this guy's really cool. But when I didn't get an internship, he was like, "Why don't you work in my lab full time?" And I was like, oh, well, okay. You're going to pay me for it. No problem. I'll do that. So he became my undergraduate advisor. But because he was starting up his lab, he didn't have any grad students. So I was basically the grad student of his lab, setting up the lab, setting up equipment, setting up logins for different things. I was the starter that had to be there.

So by the time I was a senior, we started to recruit more students. And the lab grew and everything. But by that point, I decided that I still wanted to go into drug delivery because I never got my delivery experience. And so-

Meanwhile, you're getting bamboozled step by step to be more and more into hardcore BME research.

Exactly. Exactly. So I was like, all right, I'm going to go to grad school for drug delivery. No one can tell me otherwise. This is what I'm going to do. But at this point, I had given up on the whole make-up idea and I was like, I'm just going to do something related to cancer treatment because breast cancer runs in my family. So I was like, all right, this is something that I could work towards that will contribute to me and my family and stuff. So I apply for a bunch of different programs, all of which I just found online. I didn't know anyone, any of the advisors, or really anything. I just applied on a whim.

And because I was his only student really that was full-time, he had funding, travel funding that we weren't using. So he was like, "Pick a conference and go and just present your work." And I was like, but I don't know what conference to go to. I don't know what to do. I don't know what to say. I had never done a poster, all this stuff. And he's like, "You'll figure it out, just pick a conference and go and I'll pay for it." So I went to Google again and I typed in biomaterials or something. I don't even know what keywords I typed in. But this conference that popped up was related to BMES, but it's their, I call it their sister conference. So it was cellular, molecular, and bioengineering or something. It's very tissue, and tissue regeneration, and very molecular-based. So not at all what I was doing, but I didn't know that.

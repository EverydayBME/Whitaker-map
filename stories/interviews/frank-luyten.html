Frank Luyten

Making regeneration a practical possibility

KU Leuven

What made you combine medicine with research?

I trained as a physician and did a PhD in biochemistry. Mentors helped me appreciate the science behind medical practice, but the questions also came from caring for patients.

When I entered rheumatology, there were conditions for which we had very limited treatment. That made the need for research concrete. During my career, I have seen research change what we can offer patients, and that experience is a strong reason to keep supporting it.

What did your years in the United States contribute?

I spent about eleven years at the National Institutes of Health, first as a postdoc and then as an independent investigator. It was a period when I could focus intensely on research.

I returned to Belgium in 1997 to lead rheumatology at KU Leuven. An important part of the attraction was the chance to connect a substantial research program with clinical work. Once you move deeply into basic science, maintaining contact with patients and their needs takes deliberate effort.

What does translational research mean in your work?

I have always wanted the science to contribute eventually to patient care. My main interests include regenerating damaged tissues and restoring their function, particularly cartilage, bone, and joint tissues.

That requires strong basic science as well as a route toward practical development. I have been involved in biotechnology and company formation while remaining a university professor. Those activities are connected by the aim of moving useful ideas closer to patients.

The path is long and contains many obstacles. It is not enough to have an interesting result at the laboratory bench.

Why does tissue engineering need engineers and life scientists together?

If you want to build a tissue, you need to understand the biology, but you also need to design a process that can produce it reliably. That is an engineering problem as well.

We brought people from engineering and biomedical backgrounds into a shared research environment so that they could work together daily. They need to learn one another’s language, methods, and assumptions.

The ambition is to move beyond an isolated experiment toward processes for tissue formation and manufacturing. That is why I see engineering as central to regenerative medicine.

How do you view the choice between academia and industry?

The difference is sometimes presented as larger than it really is. Academic research also involves deadlines, milestones, funding commitments, and the need to deliver results. Companies can do excellent, exciting science.

There are differences in how decisions are made and how quickly priorities can change, particularly in larger or publicly traded companies. Universities may offer more room to sustain certain questions over time, although they also depend on grants and institutional expectations.

The right choice depends partly on your personality and what kind of environment you want. Industry should not be treated as a lesser scientific path.

What does your own working week involve?

It includes patient care, managing the division and laboratory, and working with students through teaching and research. Those responsibilities have changed over my career.

At the NIH, I could concentrate almost entirely on research. Returning to a university hospital meant taking on clinical, teaching, and organizational responsibilities as well.

I discuss that balance with colleagues as their careers develop. A role does not have to contain exactly the same mix of activities throughout a person’s working life.

What should early-career researchers make of their training years?

The PhD and early postdoctoral period can offer unusually protected time to focus on a question. Later, other responsibilities often grow.

Use that time to pursue important ideas and develop your abilities. Think about the kind of work and setting you might want in the future, and look for people whose careers help you imagine those possibilities.

Role models can guide you, but you still need to make choices that fit your own interests.

What helps you decide whether a career direction is right?

Whether you generally look forward to the work. There will always be difficult periods, but if you consistently feel unhappy in a role, that deserves attention.

What fits can change with family responsibilities, personal interests, and different stages of life. I do not think a career should be only about following a title or maintaining an income. Those practical matters are real, but enjoying and believing in the work matters too.

What excites you about the future of the field?

Engineering becoming more deeply integrated with life sciences. Regenerative medicine makes the need especially clear: building functional tissues requires both biological understanding and engineering design.

There is a great deal still to do. For students who want to work across those boundaries, I see substantial opportunity.

Brittany Brumback

Making a study-abroad opportunity happen

University of Melbourne

How did you use your Whitaker grant?

I received an undergraduate grant during my sophomore year and used it to study for a semester at the University of Melbourne. I was studying biomedical engineering, and the grant supported an experience that would contribute to my development in the field.

At the time of this interview, I am a doctoral candidate at Washington University in St. Louis. Looking back, the semester abroad was an important part of becoming more independent and more willing to take on a challenge.

Why did you choose Melbourne?

My university already had established study-abroad options, but I wanted to explore something different. I needed coursework in English, and Australia interested me.

As I looked into Melbourne, its biomedical research and course offerings appealed to me. It was not an already-approved engineering exchange for us, so I petitioned to have it accepted. That took months.

Another biomedical engineering student went the following year. I am pleased that doing the work to establish the option helped make the process easier for someone else.

How important was the funding?

Very important. Tuition was covered, but travel, housing, and daily living costs were substantial. Without the grant, going would have been much more difficult.

I had applied for other funding and only found the Whitaker opportunity shortly before its deadline. People warned me that it was competitive and that applying so late might not work. I applied anyway, using recommendations that had already been prepared, and received it.

That experience taught me not to rule out an opportunity simply because it was not the one I had initially planned around.

What did you learn academically?

I took a biomechanics class that I probably would not have chosen at home. I had planned on another course, but concerns about overlap with my existing coursework led me to change.

It gave me a better appreciation for the mechanical side of the heart. I have continued more toward its electrical behavior, but understanding that the heart is both electrically and mechanically active has been valuable.

I also saw how differently biomedical engineering can be organized. The courses I encountered in Melbourne placed strong emphasis on biological signals and electrical approaches, while my home experience had emphasized other aspects of device design.

What was the biggest adjustment?

The academic expectations. My courses placed much more weight on examinations than I was used to, with less continuous assessment to show me whether I was keeping up.

I had also made assumptions about course levels based on the numbering at home. Some of the classes I chose were more advanced than I expected. I passed, but it was difficult, and I had to find tutors and classmates who could help.

The lesson was to investigate the details rather than assume that a familiar-looking course description or number means the same thing in another system.

How did you build a social life after arriving alone?

The university’s orientation events helped. There were activities around campus and the city, along with encouragement to join student organizations. Many of the people I met at the beginning remained friends through the semester.

I lived off campus in a place with many students, which gave me another way to meet people. Having those connections mattered because I arrived without an existing network.

I also met my future fiancé, who was studying there through a Brazilian international education program. The community included people from many countries and subjects, not just biomedical engineering.

Is there a memory that captures the experience for you?

Seeing Wicked in Melbourne for my birthday. I had wanted to see it for years, and my future fiancé surprised me with tickets. Going into the city, having dinner, and hearing the show with Australian accents made it a memorable evening.

It was my first time traveling internationally, and I had gone a very long way on my own. Moments like that were part of discovering a life in a new place, alongside all the academic challenges.

How did the experience affect you afterward?

I became a study-abroad adviser and made a point of explaining the academic differences to students considering Australia and New Zealand.

The experience also gave me confidence in finding support and making connections. I had to learn how to manage unfamiliar problems instead of relying on the systems I already knew. I think that growth contributed to my willingness to pursue a PhD and consider leadership roles.

What advice would you give someone planning an exchange?

Learn about the particular program, not just the country. Talk to advisers, students who have been there, and people at the host university. Ask about course levels, assessment, and what preparation is expected.

At the same time, do not choose an option only because it is easy to arrange. If there is somewhere you really want to go, investigate how to make it possible. Opportunities sometimes have to be built.

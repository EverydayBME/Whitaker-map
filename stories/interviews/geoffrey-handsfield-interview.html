Geoffrey Handsfield

From physics to the growing muscle

Auckland Bioengineering Institute, University of Auckland

Interview: 2019 · 5 minute read

What are you trying to understand about cerebral palsy?

I am interested in how its musculoskeletal effects develop as a child grows. Cerebral palsy is a neurological condition, but its effects involve muscles, bones, and movement. Those parts also influence each other: movement affects bone, bone affects muscle, and growth changes the system over time.

My approach is to combine careful experiments and imaging with computational models so we can begin to separate those interacting effects. One project looks at muscle shape and how it changes over time. Another works at the cellular scale, modeling muscle growth and regeneration.

That research plan helped me move from my Whitaker postdoc into a research fellow position at the Auckland Bioengineering Institute. I now supervise students as well as carrying out my own research.

Did you begin in biomedical engineering?

No. I studied physics, with a minor in mathematics, at East Carolina University. I was also on the varsity swim team. I wanted university to be challenging, and I wanted to understand how the world worked.

Physics gave me a great deal, but after several years I still felt that biology and physiology were a black box. As an athlete, I was especially interested in muscles. How did they work? What allowed athletes to do what they did?

At the same time, my thesis adviser encouraged me to consider biomedical applications of physics. I went to the University of Virginia for graduate study in biomedical engineering, bringing that interest in muscles together with my love of mathematics and physics.

Did changing fields mean leaving your old interests behind?

Not entirely. I was interested in cosmology as an undergraduate, and I still read about black holes and neutron stars. Mathematics can connect fields that appear very different.

One example that stayed with me was my adviser’s work applying mathematics used to study oscillations to a biomedical question about arteries. My own research moved toward muscle mechanics and MRI, but that experience helped me see that the tools you learn in one field can open doors in another.

You can change the system you study while retaining the underlying curiosity about how it works.

What was challenging about moving to New Zealand?

Moving abroad means rebuilding the social network you may have taken for granted. You arrive without the nearby friends and family who were part of everyday life. People can be welcoming, but becoming part of a settled network still takes time.

I also had to adjust to different working habits. People here take annual leave seriously, and an evening message may wait until the next working day. Coming from the United States, while still participating in an international research community, I found that adjustment more significant than I had expected.

Over time, I have felt more settled. But the social side of a move deserves attention alongside the research opportunity.

What have you valued about living there?

I have appreciated opportunities to learn about Māori culture and language and the way they contribute to New Zealand life. Around the time of this interview, the institute had participated in Māori Language Week, with signs helping people learn everyday words.

I do not mean that the history or the present is simple or perfect. But, from my perspective as someone who moved here, that effort to recognize and engage with the country’s different cultural histories has been meaningful.

What is an exciting question emerging from your group’s work?

Steph Khuu has been developing a computational model of muscle regeneration. One of the questions concerns the balance between satellite cells and fibroblasts, and how that balance affects muscle repair and fibrosis.

The model lets us explore different combinations of cells and conditions. The hope is that it could help identify useful experimental questions and, eventually, contribute to preclinical research. Understanding how to change that balance in a person is a separate challenge; the model does not make that problem disappear.

What excites me is the ability to test scenarios systematically and use the results to guide the next stage of investigation.

What possibilities outside your immediate work interest you?

Growing skeletal muscle in the laboratory is fascinating. I imagine possibilities for repairing muscle after injury or surgery, or tailoring tissue to a particular need. It is not my current area of work, and there are substantial challenges, but I keep thinking about it.

More broadly, I think well-developed computational models of human organs could contribute a great deal to drug development and clinical research. Experimental work remains important in building and testing those models. The opportunity is to use each approach where it is most informative.

How do you think about looking after yourself while doing demanding research?

We do biomedical research because we care about people and their quality of life. That should include ourselves and our colleagues.

I try to be active, eat well, and spend time away from my desk. Social connection matters too. In practice, I do not always achieve the balance I describe. It is easy to think that another hour will let you finish a few more things.

In recent years, I have tended to alternate periods of intense work with substantial trips, including climbing or surfing. More moderation would probably be useful. Recognizing the gap between your philosophy and your actual habits is part of the challenge.

Megan Guidry

Helping researchers find their footing

Auckland Bioengineering Institute, University of Auckland

Interview: 2019 · 4 minute read

What brought you to New Zealand through the Whitaker program?

I came to work on the mechanics of heart cells in Martyn Nash’s group. We were modeling what happens at the cellular level to help build better models of the heart. The long-term hope was to contribute to personalized models, using a few key measurements from an individual.

My fellowship was in the 2014–2015 year, but I started in early 2015 because the academic calendar here is different. It was a really good experience. Clearly I liked New Zealand, because I stayed.

What first drew you to biomedical engineering?

I have always been curious, and science seemed like a way to interact with the world that encouraged asking questions over and over. It is also creative. I like the arts, so science brought different parts of my personality together.

I studied biomedical engineering at the New Jersey Institute of Technology, although I almost chose architecture. Biomedical engineering appealed to me because it combined biology, human health, engineering, and creativity. It seemed challenging in a way that I liked.

How did your own experience as a researcher shape the work you do now?

During my master’s, I discovered how many skills you need and how difficult it can be to know whom to ask. Sometimes you only hear about a useful tool near the end of a project and think: that would have helped a year ago.

I became interested in helping people find those tools and develop the skills to use them. I found that more exciting than doing the research myself. It still feels closely connected to research: I am helping other people do their work well.

I work as a training coordinator for New Zealand eScience Infrastructure, which provides high-performance computing and training for researchers doing data-intensive work. I also coordinate Carpentries workshops in New Zealand. Those workshops teach foundational coding and data-management skills to researchers and graduate students.

Was there a particular moment when that career direction became clear?

After my master’s, I worked as a research assistant for a year. Toward the end, I attended a workshop on digital skills for researchers. I had been struggling to get things done in my own work, and I was excited to spend time learning different tools.

What really stood out was the atmosphere. People were encouraged to ask questions, and no question was treated as silly. I liked it so much that I stayed afterward and listened to the instructors debrief. I wanted to understand how they had created that learning environment.

One of the people there noticed my interest and knew that NeSI was hiring for a related role. She introduced me, and that is how I got involved in coordinating workshops. Having been the researcher who wanted help but felt shy about asking gives me useful empathy in this job.

What helped you look after yourself during your master’s?

At first, I was not very aware of what I needed to stay healthy through that experience. I became tired, and I often stayed quiet because I did not know whom to ask. I had that feeling that everyone else knew more than I did and that I must not let anyone see what I did not know.

Realizing that everyone is continually learning helped. If you feel isolated, reach out. People are usually very happy to help.

Doing things outside work also made a difference. I joined Toastmasters to practice public speaking. Having another interest gave me more energy for my work than simply going home exhausted. Another passion can be a real source of energy.

What excites you about the training community?

The conversations about diversity, inclusion, and creating spaces where researchers feel comfortable asking for help. At a recent eResearch conference, I met training coordinators from Australia and New Zealand working on exactly those questions.

One idea stayed with me: be the mentor you wish you had. I try to take that into my work. Supporting researchers and allowing other people to shine is exciting. Ultimately, research is about people, and I enjoy helping people.

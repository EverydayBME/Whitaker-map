Jaap van Dieën

Why staying upright is anything but simple

Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam (VU)

What are the main questions in your research?

I work in two broad areas. One concerns control of trunk posture and its relationship to low back pain. The other concerns balance during standing and walking, particularly in aging and neurological conditions.

Both involve understanding how people control movement, how that control can change, and what those changes mean clinically. My work includes meetings with students and colleagues, teaching, and administrative responsibilities. One thing I like is that there is not too much routine.

What is especially interesting about balance during walking?

We are studying how people estimate the state of their body relative to gravity and use that information to decide where to place their feet. Foot placement is an important way to correct balance and avoid falling sideways.

We have been applying predictive models to clinical populations and collaborating with a group at the University of Twente interested in exoskeleton control.

Being able to walk with an exoskeleton is valuable, but being able to do so with your hands free would be more useful still. Understanding human balance may help with that challenge.

Why is it such a difficult system to understand?

It is highly adaptable. People adjust to different mechanical environments and sensory conditions, often without thinking about it.

We still have a great deal to learn about that adaptability and how it develops. Understanding it could help us interpret clinical problems and improve assistive devices. Nature has found solutions that engineers have not yet fully reproduced.

What first drew your attention to balance?

Watching my daughter learn to walk. She was cautious and struggled at first, and seeing that process made me appreciate how difficult the task really is.

As adults, we usually take walking for granted. Watching a child learn it makes you notice the control problem. You can then recognize aspects of the same challenge in older people or patients whose balance has become less reliable.

How did your earlier work lead there?

After university, I worked at a research institute on ergonomics, particularly loading of the lower back at work. I became interested in how mechanical loading contributes to injury and how it might be prevented.

That led toward questions about recurrent and chronic low back pain and the role of motor control. We also studied balance during lifting. If you expect an object to be heavy and it is actually light, your planned movement is wrong. How do you correct for that, and how does the need to stay balanced constrain the movement?

Those questions, together with growing attention to falls in older people, helped move my research toward balance control.

What have you learned from contacting other scientists?

Early in my career, I began approaching people whose papers I admired. I discovered that they were often happy to talk, whether I contacted them before a conference or sent an email about an idea.

Some became collaborators and friends. That was an important lesson: do not assume someone is unapproachable because their work is well known.

I tell students that if they are interested in someone’s research, they should make contact. Most people enjoy discussing their work. I had to persuade myself to do it too, but it was worth it.

Do you need to move institutions to gain a different perspective?

Not necessarily. I have worked at the same university for a long time, but collaboration and visits can still expose you to different ways of thinking and working.

Spending time in another group is enriching. You see methods and assumptions that differ from your own, and that can be valuable even if your long-term position remains in one place.

How do you protect time outside work?

I enjoy the work, but I also set aside time for other activities. I like running and cycle to work. Cycling in Amsterdam demands enough attention that it helps clear my mind.

The difficulty in academia is that motivated people can always find more to do. Work is never completely finished. Getting enough sleep is a useful basic priority, and physical activity helps me with that too.

What developments in the field excite you?

Smaller sensors are making it easier to measure movement in daily life. That opens possibilities for understanding what happens beyond the laboratory and for feedback or remote-care applications.

The value is not only in having sophisticated technology. A practical, affordable way to understand and support people’s movement could be important as more people need care. That is a direction I find promising.

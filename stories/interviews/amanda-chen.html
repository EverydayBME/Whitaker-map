Background and career path

My undergraduate research PI, Danielle Benoit, and my graduate student mentor, Mike Hoffman. Danielle and Mike believed I had the potential to run before I even knew how to crawl - and pushed me to shoot my shot at every turning point in my undergrad career. They not only taught me how to carefully pipette and robustly design experiments, but also acted as my scientific and personal role models (across scientific and athletic worlds). Looking back, I can now fully appreciate the truly incredible value of their mentorship, and how essential of a role they played in getting me to where I am and where I am going. Because of them, I am always excited to invest time in mentoring younger students, whether it is in the lab through the undergraduate research program, through a campus-wide tutoring service, or via cold-email connects.

The international experience

The Whitaker Felllowship funded my tuition and living stipend for a 1-year MPhil program in the UK. Being a part of the culturally diverse and academically rich community at the Churchill College MCR (i.e. graduate study body) and the University of Cambridge Chemical Engineering & Biotechnology department helped broaden and deepen my thinking about the world of study of the sciences and humanities. At Churchill, I was the MCR Academic Chair and organized recurring graduate student seminars as well as the annual day-long Conference on Everything. Both events showcased exciting work from students at Churchill (representing a mix of degree programs from theoretical mathematics to nuclear engineering to biology to philosophy and beyond) and encouraged me to engage in technical conversations outside of my field - which is in stark contrast to my undergraduate experience, in which students socialized more closely within a specific degree program. Beyond this impressive academic spread, the vast majority of my peers hailed from countries all over the world - bringing with them exciting cultural experiences which we were all excited to share and learn from.

Work and career reflections

If I could tell a younger version of myself one thing, I would say to not be afraid to push the boundaries of science and to try out crazy ideas in the lab. In the earlier stages of my graduate career - out of fear of the increased failure rate associated with pursuing high-risk high-reward experiments - I tended to come up with project ideas that were more iterative and less exciting. I now have a greater appreciation and tolerance for risk, and aim more toward pursuing 'out-of-the-box' ideas. I believe these avenues can paradigm-shift the standard of technologies within biomedical engineering, which can subsequently be used to drive breakthroughs in human health and beyond.

What made you apply for a Whitaker Fellowship way back when?

I think my reasoning was twofold. The first thing that attracted me to applying was the fact that this was an experience to do research abroad. I have always wanted to live/work in a different country, but I never had the option to do so during my engineering studies. France in particular has always had a special attraction to me, since I studied the language and culture all throughout middle and high school. I’ve dreamed of going to France but never thought the opportunity existed. The second aspect of the Whitaker Fellowship that attracted me to applying was that I could choose my own project. When I first learned about the program, I was finishing up my Master’s degree on a tissue engineering project. Even though I was wrapping up that project, I still had a LOT of unanswered questions. I felt like I needed to do more research to answer them, but I knew wasn’t ready for a PhD yet. The Whitaker Fellowship seemed like the perfect opportunity for me to continue doing research on a topic of my choice, specifically by expanding on the research that I did in my master's, but without committing to a full PhD program just yet.

Looking back, what was something you miss the most about your Whitaker experience?

I’d say I mostly miss the work-life balance and cultural diversity of my lab in France. During that one-year fellowship, I had so many opportunities to travel throughout Europe. Not only was it easy for me to take fun weekend trips to other countries, but I got to learn about other countries’ cultures from my coworkers. My lab in France was extremely diverse. We had people from places like Lebanon, Spain, Iran, and Germany. I loved learning about their countries, their cultural perspectives, but also I really appreciated how they all brought unique perspectives to the science. The researchers in my lab had degrees from other disciplines like math, physics, and biology. I was actually one of the only engineers who worked there. That was really neat because I felt like I could contribute quite a bit more and my voice was heard, even though I was probably one of the youngest people who worked in that lab. I was able to bring my own cultural perspectives to the group, and I also felt valued as a scientist because I was an engineer. I think that cultural and educational diversity is something that’s truly unique and important for biomedical engineering.

What are you most afraid of in your career and most optimistic about?

Ultimately, I just want to be able to make an impact. I am afraid of choosing a career path that won’t let me make the impact that I know I’m capable of making. Surprisingly, there are so many career options for PhDs in biomedical engineering. I hope to choose the path that’s best for me, but also one that lets me leave the biggest mark in this world. What am I most optimistic about? I guess seeing the field of biomedical engineering growing and changing. I mean it has already changed so much over the past 10-15 years. When I was studying at Virginia Tech for my Bachelor’s, biomedical engineering was not offered as an undergraduate degree. I obviously wish that it had been, but looking back I’m grateful I was able to pick a historically traditional major and use those unique skills to guide my thought processes as a biomedical engineer today. It’s been pretty exciting to see the field garner so much interest at Tech, as it is now offered as both a minor and a major. I can’t wait to watch the field continue to evolve over the next 10-15 years.

When did you first fall in love with science? What about biomedical research?

That’s a tough one. I’ve always been in love with science. I mean when I was a kid, I would ask for things like microscopes, telescopes, and science experiment books as birthday presents. I guess my curiosity about our world and having a knack for exploring the unknown has always been a part of me. As for biomedical research, I’d say I probably realized that particular passion of mine when I was maybe a sophomore or junior in college. I declared my major to be Materials Science and Engineering because I loved the idea of getting a degree in a field that would let me understand the properties of matter and everything around me. In one of my intro classes, our professor was explaining the concept of biomimetic materials, materials which are developed using inspiration from nature. I remember one lecture where he was teaching us about tensile strength, and he mentioned that spider silk was stronger than any man-made fiber – it is even stronger than steel. That floored me. What other materials does nature have that can be leveraged in the design of new materials for our own applications? Well, it turns out hedgehog spines are naturally great shock absorbers, and honeycombs make the most storage possible with the least amount of material. When I realized that scientists and engineers could create amazing materials just by mimicking nature, I really got interested in the intersection of biology and engineering.

Prasad Babarenda

Making models useful in the clinic

Auckland Bioengineering Institute, University of Auckland

What is the main question behind your research?

I work on the mechanics of large tissue deformations: how tissues move and change shape. Much of my work involves physics-based computational models, especially in breast biomechanics.

Breast cancer diagnosis uses several imaging methods, but those images are taken in different positions. A mammogram compresses the breast while the patient is standing; an MRI is obtained with the patient lying on their front; ultrasound or surgery may take place with them on their back. The images provide different information, and the tissue has moved between them.

We develop models to predict that movement. Our aim is an automated workflow that takes medical images, identifies the tissues of interest, builds a patient-specific anatomical model, and predicts where those tissues will move during a procedure. We have implemented a prototype workflow at Auckland City Hospital.

Why is automation important?

A model has to fit into the clinical environment to be useful. It is not enough to produce an interesting result in a research setting. We need to communicate with clinicians and understand how a tool could work within their existing systems.

Sometimes one part of a larger workflow can help immediately, even before the full mechanics model is ready for routine use. We try to identify those useful pieces and get them into clinicians’ hands. Translating research involves a different set of challenges from doing the underlying science.

How does that thinking apply to the heart?

We are interested in better tools for understanding heart disease, particularly heart failure. Images show motion and anatomy, but an image alone cannot tell you how stiff the tissue is. You also need information about loading, such as pressure measurements.

Computational models bring those measurements together. We want to estimate stiffness and compare how it varies in healthy and diseased hearts. Eventually, we would like to connect those mechanical changes to changes in tissue microstructure, including collagen.

The broader goal is more informative diagnosis, before disease reaches a stage at which options are very limited. That is the motivation for combining measurements in one physical framework.

You also work on lungs. What does that involve?

We want to distinguish changes associated with normal aging from changes associated with disease. That means studying both structure and function: what the lung looks like, how its mechanical properties vary, and how it deforms.

We built a twelve-camera imaging system for controlled inflation experiments on intact rat lungs. We can measure pressure and track movement of the surface as the lung inflates. Optical coherence tomography also lets us examine structures beneath the surface.

These experiments provide detailed information that we cannot obtain in the clinic. That makes them useful for testing and validating computational models. Instrumentation and modeling support each other: better measurements let us ask better questions about the models.

What first brought you into biomedical engineering?

I injured my ACL playing soccer during my last year at school. I was sixteen and still growing, so I had to wait before reconstruction. During that year, my knee repeatedly gave way.

Before that, I had been thinking about aerospace or mechanical engineering. The injury made unresolved medical problems feel much more immediate, and biomedical engineering began to look like a direction where I could contribute.

But choosing the degree did not immediately tell me which kind of work I would enjoy. I had to try different things.

How did you discover the work that suited you?

As an undergraduate, I approached different groups and asked whether I could work in their laboratories. I tried cartilage research and a neuroimaging project related to stroke. An area can sound exciting from the outside and still turn out not to be the kind of day-to-day work you enjoy.

Programming was the opposite. I initially disliked it, but once I applied it to a problem that meant something to me, I found it exciting. Summer work involving programming helped clarify that. My final-year project involved breast modeling, which led into my PhD.

That is why I recommend actively exploring. Go to seminars outside your immediate field. An idea from somewhere else may help with your own problem. You have to seek those opportunities rather than assume your first impression tells you everything.

Where would you like your career to lead?

I would like to become an academic. I enjoy research, supervising students, and teaching, and I have had opportunities to develop those skills here. I even enjoy aspects of writing grants: constructing a project and deciding how to take it forward.

Being able to build projects and guide their direction appeals to me. Opportunities and funding matter, but academia is the path I would like to pursue.

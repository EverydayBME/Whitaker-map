Azade Allahyari

From medical devices to making new materials

University of Melbourne

Interview: 2019 · 4 minute read

What are you working on in Melbourne?

I am doing my master’s thesis in tissue engineering. The project involves decellularized tissue: using the material that remains after cells are removed as a possible scaffold or component of a biomaterial.

We want to investigate how that material behaves, how it works with cells, and whether it could be useful for biofabrication. I am interested in the possibility of developing a material for three-dimensional printing, but the work is still exploratory. First we have to understand its properties.

At the moment, I am completing the hospital and laboratory requirements before starting that part of the project. I am looking forward to working with the tissue and doing the mechanical testing.

How did you first choose biomedical engineering?

At eighteen, I did not have a clear idea of becoming a scientist. In Iran, the entrance examination was a major focus. After getting a good result, I had the opportunity to choose a field.

Biomedical engineering appealed to me because it combined the body with engineering, although I did not yet fully understand what the work would be like. I completed my bachelor’s with the intention of going into industry. I did not plan to keep studying.

What did your first job teach you?

I worked as a product specialist for a medical-device company, including time in operating rooms supporting equipment used in minimally invasive surgery. I enjoyed seeing the clinical environment and understanding how the instruments were used.

It could be demanding: several devices might be involved, and you needed to understand them and respond when something went wrong. That experience gave me confidence and a much clearer idea of working life.

Over time, though, I felt I had stopped learning in the way I wanted. I knew the products, but I wanted to help develop something new rather than only work with equipment that already existed. That was the turning point that made me consider a master’s and possibly a PhD.

Why did you choose to study in Germany?

I looked for an affordable program taught in English and found a biofabrication program that interested me. It combined areas I wanted to explore, including tissue engineering and three-dimensional printing.

I particularly enjoyed biotechnology and tissue engineering courses, along with practical sessions that introduced different laboratories and equipment. Studying again felt different because I now had work experience and a clearer reason for being there.

Financially, it was challenging. Changes in the exchange rate reduced what my savings could cover, so I worked alongside my studies, including university research support and hospitality work. Working in hospitality also helped me improve my German.

How did the opportunity in Melbourne come about?

I learned that the German university had links with Melbourne and opportunities to support students going there. I looked at research groups and contacted a professor whose work on decellularization interested me.

We discussed a possible project, and I applied for a scholarship with the supporting documents and supervisor input. I also had an opportunity to meet people from the collaboration during a summer school.

Coming to Australia was a new experience. Using English has been easier for me than using German, and having friends from my earlier university years here has helped with the transition.

What obstacles have you faced in pursuing international study?

Money has been one. Access to opportunities has been another. I have encountered programs or resources that were unavailable to me because of my nationality or location. Sometimes it was difficult to understand all the reasons behind a restriction, but the effect was that I could not take part.

Being away from family is also hard. You want to spend time with them, and pursuing these opportunities has meant making sacrifices. Support from my mother and sister has been very important.

Who helped build your confidence in science?

My mother. She was a mathematics teacher, and when I was younger she spent time helping me become interested in the subject. She has supported me throughout these years.

My sister has also been a strong source of emotional support. Even when you are doing the studying and moving yourself, that encouragement matters.

What would you like to do next?

I would like to pursue a PhD in an area such as tissue engineering or biofabrication. I am exploring programs, while also keeping other possibilities open.

At the moment, I imagine returning to industry in a research and development role. The kind of industry work I want is different from my first job: I want to contribute to new materials or technologies. My plans may change, but I now know that continuing to learn and create is important to me.

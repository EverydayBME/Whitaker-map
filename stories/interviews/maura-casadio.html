“I think is really important on this field is to build a common language between engineers or technical people that work on the biomedical field and people that work on clinics. Medical doctor, physical therapist, psychologist. And having a common ground, a common language. And so this you can start building up from the courses at your university in general. I'm teaching two different classes; one is rehabilitation engineering and on that, I always invite expert from a different field to discuss with the students. And that's when I propose my strategies, I try for at least some of them to couple. Engineering students with physical therapy students or psychologists, so that there can be an exchange at their level that improve their research and improve the building of this common ground and language.”

Can you maybe tell me a bit more about how you got interested in biomedical engineering initially, like before you went to university?

I think that maybe a part is due to the fact that my father got a stroke when he was relatively young. So I think he was my age now, so 44. I saw how this was a deep life change for him. And I thought that this was, for a lot of people a neural affair. Because there are a lot of things that you can do. Both for recovery but both also if you cannot recovery, for having an independent and active life. There were a lot of things that were missing and I thought that technology and research could do a lot for people as my father…And a little bit also from the scientific point of view, I think is also, forgive me the word, fascinating to understand what happens in these cases. Because when we study healthy people behavior, we aim to understand how our brain works. And when you start working with people, in which something in the central nerval system is not working anymore because they had an injury or effect of the disease, then you can see what is modification lead the level of behavior. So your understanding of the brain on one side; I don't know if I'm able to explain myself but on one side is helping, on building a common knowledge on the brain that will be helpful for understanding the disease.

Can you maybe elaborate on kind of the roles of mentors in your career? And can you maybe name a few mentors that kind of influenced your career and helped shaped where you've gone and helped you?

I think that most of the things that I know and good part of what I am as researcher is due to my mentor here over in [inaudible] that was Petro Morasa. From him I learned a lot about what means, studying a problem, do research and I like it. …We had a lot of rejections so, my first paper we employ two years to publish. What happened was that when we tried to published, they [the reviewers] were killing our paper. Constantly. So it took a while before starting to be into the field and people recognize the work that was done here. He was always positive, always supportive and teaching me everything from, really the basics. How you plan an experiment, how you write a paper, how you answer the review. He was really passionate and really positive. Really positive attitude. And you could see that he was loving his job. He is loving his job because he's still doing research.

Okay now I want to focus on your motivations for going abroad. So can you tell me more about what... and how you ended up there in northwestern Chicago?

This I think is really human part of the story. I didn't want to go abroad. In Italy people don't move a lot…Me, coming from University of Pisa, I was already the foreign one. But when I finished my PhD and I went to the hospital and I felt that I want to go back to research, I apply for a position in IAT. At that moment they made me understanding that, for work in an international research institution, as they want to build the IAT, it was better having an international experience.

What skills you learned, abroad that you might have not been able to?

In the US we had these lab meetings in which you have a presentation and you are supposed not to go further your [inaudible 00:20:04]. No you just start, you don't have to finish your presentation because everyone will ask you question. And could be the last undergraduate there that really challenge you on that. I thought that that was beautiful because it was making your research better. It was helping everyone to have more critical thought about the work. And also from the personal view of also the presenter. You learn not only to make your research better but also to accept critique, to answer the critics. So this part, the lab meetings in which everyone is asking and participating and everyone is the same level. And everyone can ask or [inaudible] they want. I really like it. That was a key point of the way in which their research and their discussion is conducted in the states that, I really like it. …Another difference that I find out is that at least the [inaudible] was, there was a lot of freedom. Go sideways and... Maybe you say that they do a lot of fishing expedition.

What you're struggling with nowadays in your current role?

With everything. With everything. More so, the first is all the teaching duty that here are a lot. And it's what I wanted to do but sometimes, time is really too much. And then I feel that I like mentoring student but it's challenging…Because all of us, we were in place in which we had doubt about our research or we were not in the right path and don't know what we were doing. I appreciate how my mentor solve this problem. Sometimes for me it's a struggle, helping student with different character, with different attitude. With someone you are... it's easier to guide them to overcome this, not technical, but these psychological difficulty. With someone else, really there is more difficulty on the language and I think it's really challenging, you know. To go through, this part of the mentorship. And then there is the research part that is again what you mentioned before. So the fact that there is a around us a lot of pressure for, send out a lot of research product and do that as fast as you can. On the other side, you have idea that you want to go in another direction. You want to explore. Then be able not to let your publication go down, but in the meantime try to follow the path that in that moment you feel that, is really promising, is really leading you where you want to go. That moment sometimes are moment in which you struggle. And then you want to do all this. So you want to do your teaching well, mentoring your PhD student well, having a good international relationship, a project in common. Having a good research record. We work a lot with a lot of hospitals, so translate your research with that hospital. Plus you have your family, that has a really important point in your life. The primary point.

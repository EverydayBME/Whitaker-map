Martyn Nash

Opening the black box of the body

Auckland Bioengineering Institute, University of Auckland

What connects the different projects in your research?

Soft tissue mechanics is the common thread. I teach in engineering at the University of Auckland, and I do my research in the Auckland Bioengineering Institute. We use mathematics, physics, and computation to understand how biological tissues behave.

In heart research, we combine three-dimensional images with pressure measurements to build personalized biomechanical models. We want to understand properties such as the elasticity of heart muscle, its contraction, and its relaxation.

A measurement such as ejection fraction tells you about the heart’s overall performance, but it also depends on the heart’s size and geometry. Engineering lets us account for those features and ask more specific questions about the tissue itself. The aim is to improve how we monitor disease and, potentially, responses to treatment.

How does the same approach help with breast cancer imaging?

Different scans show different things, and they are taken with the body in different positions. In mammography, the breast is compressed. In MRI, the person lies on their front. In ultrasound, they may lie on their back.

A suspicious region therefore moves between scans. We use biomechanical models to predict that movement and help clinicians connect the information. If a lesion is visible in an MRI, where should someone look for it with ultrasound when guiding a biopsy?

The challenge is to customize our models to the tissues and imaging methods involved. I have worked on the heart, breast, skin, tongue, and stomach. The engineering principles connect them, but each organ presents different problems.

What is most exciting about working with clinicians?

It takes me out of my computer-lab comfort zone. Clinicians already have a wealth of information, and we can help them get more from it. Talking with cardiologists, understanding their questions, and providing information that may support their decisions is tremendously stimulating.

Machine learning can reveal interesting patterns, but I think combining it with physics-based modeling offers further possibilities. For me, the exciting part is helping clinicians make more specific, personalized use of information they already collect.

Did you always intend to become a biomedical engineer?

No. At school I liked mathematics, physics, computers, and finding out how things worked. Biology felt more like learning facts and giving them back. I wanted to open the black box and understand the mechanisms inside it.

I entered engineering because I wanted to solve problems with mathematics and computing. During my degree I discovered mechanics and its applications to biology. Suddenly I could use the subjects I enjoyed to help people. Good mentors opened that world to me, and I did a PhD in heart mechanics.

I did not have an academic career mapped out. Near the end of my PhD, I imagined working as a programmer for a medical company in London. Then an opportunity at Oxford came through my network, and I decided to try it.

What changed during your time at Oxford?

I moved from computational mechanics into cardiac electrophysiology, from a dry lab into a physiology laboratory. The heart connected the two, but otherwise it was a substantial change.

I worked with clinicians and measured electrical activity in operating theaters. Standing there as an engineer, with equipment connected to a patient’s heart, was an extraordinary learning experience. I had to learn a different scientific language and communicate across disciplines.

I also discovered how much I enjoyed working with graduate students. That experience helped me see academia as a career. Returning to New Zealand and joining the Bioengineering Institute then made sense.

What did the Whitaker fellows contribute to your group?

Several worked on fundamental questions about cardiac muscle. We have colleagues who build instruments to measure muscle behavior and others who specialize in mathematical models. Bringing those approaches together helps us understand contraction, energy use, and how cellular behavior contributes to whole-organ function.

The fellows helped develop and extend mathematical models using those measurements. Some work focused on cellular processes; other work embedded those descriptions in three-dimensional muscle and ventricular models. That is discovery science: opening the box and asking how it works.

How do you approach work and family life?

I love the work, so it can be difficult to put down. I sometimes describe it as a paid hobby, although my family would also point out the long hours and the stress. Both perspectives are real.

I protect time with my family and take a substantial summer break from the office. I remain available for important questions, but students understand that routine matters can wait. Flexibility helps, and so does recognizing that there are only so many hours in a day. Overcommitting is something I have to watch.

What advice would you give a student still finding their direction?

Never stop asking questions. I want to learn something from every presentation, whether it is by an undergraduate or a specialist at a conference. If you do not understand, ask.

Follow your curiosity, and do not expect someone else to identify your entire career for you. Find work that stimulates you, stay open to changing direction, and keep doors open. My move into electrophysiology was not part of a carefully fixed plan, but it became one of the most valuable experiences of my career.

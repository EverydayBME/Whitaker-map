Stacy Skaalure

Making materials—and room for a life beyond the lab

Imperial College London

Interview: 29 April 2019 · 4 minute read

What brought you to Imperial College London?

I came as a Whitaker fellow to work in the Stevens Group on biomaterials for tissue engineering, drug delivery, and regenerative medicine. My original project involved hydrogels and ways to assess cells differentiating toward bone and cartilage.

During the first year, I applied for a Marie Curie fellowship. Receiving it allowed me to extend my time in the group. These projects are not necessarily finished in two years, and I had become involved in several different areas that I wanted to explore further.

I also liked working with people from different disciplines and backgrounds. It was a stimulating place to learn.

How has your research developed?

I moved somewhat from tissue engineering toward hydrogels for controlled delivery. One idea is a material that could be injected and then release a therapeutic over time as it degrades, perhaps in response to light or enzymes.

I also work with light-responsive materials for creating three-dimensional environments in which to grow cells. Those environments could be useful for tissue engineering or for small tissue models used to study biology and test drugs.

My contribution is the biomaterial itself: understanding what cells need and using chemistry to create an environment that supports them. The larger projects depend on many people with different expertise.

What does an ordinary day look like?

It is a mixture of synthesis, cell culture, meetings, writing, and helping students. As a more senior postdoc, I spend time discussing experiments, reviewing progress, and working with PhD and master’s students.

I still do laboratory work, but I cannot do every part of every project myself. The group is a network of interconnected projects. Moving between the laboratory, the office, and meetings is a normal part of the day.

Why did you choose biomedical engineering?

I had too many interests and found it hard to choose one. Biomedical engineering offered a way to bring together biology, chemistry, mathematics, physics, and making things.

There was also a personal connection. Members of my family had undergone joint replacements, and seeing what those surgeries involved helped motivate my interest in cartilage regeneration during my PhD.

I think I am fundamentally an engineer. I like building and making things with my hands. Working with chemistry and polymers gives me a way to do that while pursuing questions that matter for health.

How have you learned to set boundaries around work?

It is difficult, particularly in an environment full of people who push themselves hard. During my PhD, I sometimes pushed too far. I have had to learn to recognize that and adjust.

Simple routines help. I like cooking dinner, which gives me a reason to leave at a reasonable time. My partner and I also check in with each other about when we are coming home.

Stepping away is part of being productive and creative. I enjoy films, books, and cooking, and having different interests helps me make that space.

How did cooking become such a big part of your life?

I became vegetarian near the end of undergraduate study. Graduate school was also the first time I had to organize all my own meals, so I had quite a lot to learn.

I discovered that I really enjoyed it. Friends started asking for recipes, and eventually I put them online so I could share them more easily. It became a creative outlet as well as a way to look after myself.

I grew up partly in Indonesia, so Southeast Asian food is an influence. Traveling gives me more ideas. I enjoy finding something new and bringing it into what I cook at home.

What did moving abroad teach you?

Even after moving around earlier in life, arriving in London was overwhelming. I did not know people here, and in the first few days I wondered whether I had made a mistake.

Looking back, I have made lasting friends and learned an enormous amount. Becoming part of a different scientific community broadened my sense of what was available to me. It helped me stop thinking of opportunities outside the United States as belonging to some separate world.

It also built confidence: you can arrive somewhere unfamiliar and find a place where you contribute.

What would you tell your younger self?

Do not overload yourself trying to please everyone. I was studying biomedical engineering while also pursuing premedical requirements, and eventually I reached a point where I needed to let something go.

It is hard to make those decisions when other people have expectations for you. But trying to do everything can leave you exhausted rather than moving toward the work that suits you.

What are you considering after your postdoc?

I have decided that running an academic laboratory may not be the right path for me. I am exploring possibilities including startups, entrepreneurship, consulting, and science policy.

The next step is not fully settled. But I would like to use what I have learned in a broader scientific role. There is more than one way to have a successful career or make a contribution.

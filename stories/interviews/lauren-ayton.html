Good morning Dr. Ayton, may you please introduce yourself and your position?

Hi, I’m Dr. Lauren Ayton. I am a senior research fellow at the University of Melbourne. Actually, I'm a “Driving Research Momentum” Fellow. I have a joint appointment between ophthalmology and optometry at the University. Therefore, I am split across two different campuses. I spend half of my week at the University and then half of my week at the Eye and Ear campus. My lab is called the Vision Optimisation Group.

What is the focus of the Vision Optimisation Group?

Basically, we are all about trying to improve vision for people that have got either blindness or severe vision impairment. We investigate things like bionic eyes, gene therapy, and assistive technologies like smart glasses and audio devices. Also, we are very interested in the natural history of retinal diseases.

What are some of the main projects in your portfolio right now?

The main one at the moment is gene therapy. I am working a lot on inherited retinal diseases, which comprise the leading cause of blindness in working aged adults. Historically, there was absolutely no treatment. You diagnose someone and then say, "Look, I'm really sorry. You're going to go blind. I don't know how long it will take. I don't know what it will look like for you, but there's nothing we can do." Now we are trying to learn more about natural history, so that we can say, "It is likely that you'll have X number of years," and we are trying to come up with treatments.

Our current gene therapy program is for a particular type retinal degeneration that is very prevalent in Australia and in Asia. We are basically trying to cure it. It still feels weird using that word, because being in the field, you hate using that word, but that is honestly what gene therapy is trying to do: to actually just stop in its tracks. It's pretty exciting!

What does your day-to-day generally look like?

It varies a lot, as I am part of a larger team. I don't have the preclinical lab skills to develop the actual treatment, so I spend a lot of time meeting with the scientists that are developing the gene therapy. The whole program's headed by an ophthalmologist named Dr. Tom Edwards, who worked at Oxford for a number of years on gene therapy programs there. We work quite closely together, but at the moment, my particular role is getting patients in that have this disease. My team basically works to learn more about them. We measure their vision, we take photos and scans of their eyes, we ask them questions about what their life is like, and we build background information about them in preparation for a clinical trial where we can start to try and stop the disease.

What interested you in science in the first place?

I think I had a slightly different path to a lot of people. I was always interested in science. My mom is a teacher, and my dad is an engineer. I was always between the two. I was very interested in literature and also in the sciences, but I actually wanted to be a journalist. During the first few years of high school, that was what I was going to do. Then, I had an amazing science teacher in year 10 who just changed my world. I remember him coming in and it was when the human genome project was underway. He came in one day and said, "Oh, we were meant to talk about this topic today, but I'm not going to because this is so game changing. This is what we should talk about." He sat there for the whole lesson and just talked about the fact that we are going to know the whole genetic code of humans and then we can change it. It's giving me goosebumps. I can still remember sitting there going, "That's what I want to do. I want to be part of that, the things that are changing so quickly." But then I had this thing- I wanted to be a clinician, but I didn't really want to work in a hospital. I began looking at different allied health options. I went into optometry. Then, I did my PhD. My postdoc was in traumatic brain injury and vision loss. Finally, I moved into blindness and vision restoration.

But it's funny, and I always laugh because it's quite ironic that I'm back in gene therapy now given that's what started it. That was what I was interested in. It has come full circle. I think the fact that I have always been interested in all the other areas makes sense that I gravitated towards multidisciplinary research because I'm always wanting to learn more. So, working with engineers is great. I learn stuff every single day that I didn't know before. That's what I want.

What kind of work transpires between your clinical team and the technical engineering team?

In all of the different projects I am working on, it is a very close relationship. For example, the bionic eye project was a huge project. There were over 250 researchers at one point. For that sort of project to work you have to be on the same page. We would have meetings where it would be biomedical engineers, surgeons, clinicians, computer scientists, and material scientists. Everyone was contributing their input into what the device had to look like, how to get it into the eye, and what it should do once it is in the eye. That was pretty exciting, being involved in all the processes rather than just being given something at the end and saying, "Test this." Actually, being involved from the start means you can help direct it and make sure that it works in the eye.

What are some of the key decisions that you have made during your career?

When I finished my optometry training, I had the decision of, "Do I go down the research path or the clinical path?" I really love working with patients and with people. I remember having a conversation with someone about the fact that if I went into research, I would obviously still have clinical skills, but I wouldn't be the same clinician than someone working 100% in clinic. I had to make that decision.

Then, after I did my PhD, I looked towards taking a postdoc in the US and it was in pediatrics. My PhD had been in pediatrics, so that was a key decision. I really wanted to go to the US and move there, but I had a partner at the time who couldn't get a visa. That was another big turning point – whether I went or stayed – and thankfully at the same time, the job with Bionic Vision Australia came up and it was just perfect timing, so that was the right decision. It's funny how at the time it was very stressful and you're not sure if you've made the right call, but then, retrospectively, it was the right thing to do.

What balances do you manage between life at home and work?

I've got a three-and-a-half-year-old who is a very active boy, so a lot of my time is dedicated to him. I was at a talk the other day and they said, "The phrase work life balance is outdated. It should be work life integration," and I really liked that because that's what it is for me. I'm very lucky that my husband is incredibly supportive and, basically, we just juggle things. For me, it is really important that I can finish work at a reasonable time to pick up my son from daycare so I can play with him; but then for me that also means I work after he goes to bed. That's what works for us. Yes, I think it's important to have both. I have got some colleagues who are amazing scientists, but at the risk of burnout because it is a very consuming career. I've been very lucky that I have got good friends and family that keep me distracted and give me other things to think about.

Speaking of burnout, how do you avoid it?

I think it's a roller coaster. At certain times during the year, especially when grants are due, work does become all consuming, and so that's what I'm in the midst of at the moment. I'm running a fellowship, and it's a lot of time. But I think the nice thing with academia is that it is flexible. So when things are a bit quieter then you can try and fill in other parts to keep your sanity. Otherwise, I like running and things like that. I try to get stress relief out that way and those sorts of little things. You try and find the time when you can.

What was your experience like as a hosting mentor for a Whitaker research fellow?

I think [international education] is so invaluable. Nick Apollo was the fellow that I supervised, and he is brilliant. He's a very, very smart engineer who's highly passionate about what he does. He was a pleasure to work. I hope it was great for him, but it was amazing for our team because he brought in a whole different perspective. He had experience with different techniques. He had worked with different people. To be able to bring that into our group was amazing.

I've actually just returned from living in the States for a couple of years. I was in upstate New York, in Utica. It's a beautiful spot, but again, it taught me so much. It's just a totally different way about thinking about so many different things, including science, but everything else as well, and that, I think, makes your work better.

Nick did an amazing project with us. He was an engineer and hadn't really worked with patients. We threw him in the deep end, and he was involved with seeing the people that had the bionic eye implants. He was taking images and analyzing image data and all sorts of different projects within the larger program. Nick became very interested in the materials that we were working with for the bionic eye. He went and did his PhD on that in the Department of Physics, and then he returned to the US into academia and now he is in industry. He's doing very amazing things.

Where do you see the future of your field going?

I am writing grants at the moment and you always write the first few sentences about how it is so important, and it is game changing. I felt like I actually honestly really believe it because it's changing so quickly.

When I finished optometry, these patients had no options. There are now three bionic eyes that have been regulatory approved. The first ever in vivo gene therapy for any disease was for ophthalmology for a very similar condition, so it's totally game changing.

Things like CRISPR gene editing and STEM cells are around the corner. I honestly think that within several years, there is going to be a range of options for these people. That's pretty exciting and I am really excited about long term combination treatments. I'm obviously very interested in biomedical engineering and medical devices, but I think there's potential for those to be used in combination with pharmaceuticals or gene therapy and STEM cells. And that's pretty cool again, because that's bringing in all these different groups to work on treatments to save science, so that's where I hope it'll go.

What is most interesting or rewarding about your work?

All of the general media make it sound like [the bionic eye] is just going to restore vision and it doesn't. It’s very basic rudimentary visual cues. People get little phosphenes of lights, which you can use to map the scene.

What really fascinates me are the unique experiences that people report. Some of the people we had implanted talked about seeing little colors and flashes in different areas. They were really enjoying those really interesting visual phenomena. So, it's that enjoyment of vision, rather than it just being a utilitarian thing, that you need it to work around. It was the joy of seeing again.

I’ll share one of my favorite stories. Part of my research is designing new outcome measures for these sorts of trials. We design obstacle courses, objects, sorting tasks, and all these sorts of things. The best outcome for one of our participants was when she used to go and get her coffee in the morning. She wasn't sure where the barista was along the bench, so she would just go up to the bench and he would come over to her. She was saying that with the device on, she could locate him. She could work out where he was and enjoy that simple social interaction of knowing that she was pointing towards him and knowing that they are having face-to-face connection. We were saying at the time, “How do you measure that? How do you measure that enjoyment and fun of being able to find something?” So, we're still working on that. That's one of my ultimate goals: how do you sum all of that experience?

Do you have any heroes in the field?

Oh gosh, I have so many. I guess the obvious one in sensory restoration would be Graeme Clark, father of cochlear implant here in Australia. He is a huge hero because of what he went through at the start. I think it was what a lot of researchers goes through where people just doubted him. They didn't think that it was really going to work. I've spoken a lot with people that worked with him on the original cochlear implant studies, and they say that their grants were knocked back, publications were questioned, and now it's helped hundreds of thousands of people. I think it is 350,000 people around the world. You can see those beautiful videos of kids getting their hearing back straight away, so obviously that success story is just amazing. The other thing I really respected is that they obviously managed to successfully commercialize it so that it could get out to patients, but without, I guess having to curtail the research, which continues now. The Bionics Institute in Melbourne are still working on improving the cochlear implant every day. That's really powerful too, that the ecosystem's still so innovative.

Oh gosh, heroes. So, the other two are actually more local, so probably two of the mentors that I work with. Robin Guymer and Penny Allen are both professors. They're both ophthalmic surgeons that work at the Eye and Ear Hospital, Centre for Eye Research Australia. They are amazing in the research that they do and their clinical input, but also that work life balance you were talking about, so really having work-life integration.

What is next for you?

My plan at the moment is to basically build my team up. I've only been back for about six months now in Australia. I'm trying to build this team up with people that have got similar dreams and aspirations, I guess, and then cure blindness.

Andreas Hierlemann

Building tools—and the collaborations that make them useful

ETH Zurich

What does your group build?

We develop engineering tools for biological and biomedical questions. One major area is high-resolution microelectrode arrays, which record electrical signals from cells such as neurons and heart cells.

Another is microfluidic systems containing small tissues. These are often described as body-on-a-chip systems: controlled settings that reproduce selected functions of tissues or organs.

The aim is to give researchers ways to measure and investigate biological behavior that would otherwise be difficult to access.

What can a microelectrode array tell you?

Cells or a piece of tissue can be placed on an array, and the electrodes record the electrical activity they produce. With a dense array, we can examine that activity at a very fine scale.

Researchers can compare different cell preparations or investigate how activity changes under particular conditions. Patient-derived cell models are one possible application, including studies of disease mechanisms and responses to drugs.

Developing the technology has taken many years. Reaching a point where it is useful to other researchers, and can be commercialized, is the result of sustained engineering work rather than a single invention.

How did you move from chemistry into this field?

I trained in physical chemistry and was drawn to physical explanations and mathematical models. Chemical sensors became the focus of my PhD, and during my postdoc at Texas A&M I became more involved in the technological side of microsensors.

At ETH, I joined a group developing semiconductor-based sensors and helped extend its work toward chemical sensing. Over time, that led into increasingly biological applications.

Moving between physical chemistry and sensor technology was fairly smooth. Moving into neuroscience was more challenging, because learning the methods is only part of entering a new field. You also need to understand its questions and establish relationships.

What makes a good collaboration between an engineer and a biologist?

Both sides need to be willing to develop something together. An emerging technology is not necessarily a finished instrument that works immediately in every situation.

I look for collaborators who are interested enough in the possibilities to help refine it. Their scientific questions and practical feedback can guide the engineering, while our technology can enable measurements they could not otherwise make.

Nearby collaborators make conversation and troubleshooting easier. International collaborations can work well too, often with an initial visit for training followed by continued discussion and development.

Did you always want a scientific career?

Not in a single-minded way. Sport was a major part of my life through my PhD, particularly artistic gymnastics. I spent a great deal of time training.

The fascination with a scientific career developed more gradually. I also had to find my way through aspects of the research environment that I did not enjoy, including strong personalities and competition over ideas.

My decision to continue became clearer after the PhD rather than before it.

Did gymnastics teach you anything useful for research?

It taught me to organize my time, focus on the task in front of me, and work consistently toward a goal. Combining substantial training with study and laboratory work required efficiency.

Those skills are useful in science, although sport is not the only place to learn them. The broader lesson is that an interest outside research can contribute something to how you work, rather than merely taking time away from it.

How do you maintain that connection to life outside science?

I think it is important not to become completely immersed in the scientific world. Keep your relationships with friends and family, and do not give up the activities that matter to you.

For me, sport provides that balance. Travel and meetings can make a regular schedule difficult, and there are busy periods when work takes more time. But when the pressure eases, you should also use that opportunity to reclaim personal time.

What does a meaningful academic career look like to you?

It is not defined only by papers or impact factors. Science is done by people, and a large part of the responsibility is helping people develop.

That means taking their difficulties seriously while also challenging them. A PhD should help someone learn to organize themselves, make decisions, and eventually lead others. Supporting that development is a substantial part of the job.

What advice would you give your younger self?

Follow work you believe in, but accept that a career cannot be planned completely. Opportunities can appear in places you did not expect. Before I moved to Switzerland, it was not something I had imagined doing.

Do serious work, build relationships with people you trust, and make decisions as opportunities arise. There is no guarantee that every choice will work out, but worrying constantly about things outside your control will not make the path predictable.

Jess Snedeker

Following the project, not a fixed career plan

ETH Zurich

What is the focus of your research group?

We study orthopedic biomechanics and musculoskeletal disease. We are interested in how mechanics interacts with biology and how that knowledge might improve healing.

One part of the work involves growing tissues in the laboratory. We do that primarily to understand the science, rather than to produce a tissue for immediate transplantation. By seeing when tissues develop well and when they do not, we can investigate mechanisms of healing and the conditions that influence them.

That knowledge can also inform biomaterial design. The broader goal is to understand how materials and cells interact and whether we can encourage a better healing response.

How does your joint appointment work?

I have appointments at ETH Zurich and the University of Zurich, including the medical faculty’s Department of Orthopedics. Alongside my own group, I have responsibilities for the basic science program and research infrastructure in orthopedics.

The institutions have different structures, so the administrative work varies. But the central activities for me are science and education. I enjoy teaching as well as doing research, even when other responsibilities compete for time.

Did you always plan to become a professor?

No. I studied mechanical engineering at Lehigh and then worked in industry for three years, doing research and development at W. L. Gore & Associates.

I liked that work. I began considering graduate school partly because I was interested in project management and noticed that many people in those roles had PhDs.

During my master’s at Penn State, I worked on exercise countermeasures for astronauts. The question was whether ways of attaching someone to a treadmill in microgravity reproduced the loading of running on Earth. It was a fascinating project, and I enjoyed the scientific process: designing experiments, collecting data, and making sense of it.

What drew you toward academic research?

I appreciated the freedom to follow a question because it was scientifically interesting, rather than only because it met an immediate commercial need.

I moved to Switzerland for my PhD, gained experience with numerical methods and tissue experiments, and then continued into a postdoc with more cell biology and regenerative medicine. My interests moved across scales, but I kept an engineering perspective: quantitative experiments and questions about mechanics.

Eventually, I began an independent group. That was the outcome of following work I enjoyed, rather than a goal I had set at the start of my education.

How did your industry experience affect later career decisions?

It meant I knew there was another kind of work I could enjoy. When I looked for positions, I considered both academic and industry opportunities. An academic job was not the only acceptable outcome.

That made the decision less frightening. I had already changed direction once and knew that changing course was possible. Industry can offer a rich scientific life too, depending on the role and what you want from it.

There are practical issues, of course. Returning to graduate study meant a substantial drop in income for me. Those decisions become more complicated when other people depend on your earnings.

How did you choose your PhD opportunity in Zurich?

I followed the project. Was it something I could learn from? Would I be interested enough to invest my working days in it? Did the environment have the resources to let me do it properly?

I also liked the idea of living in Switzerland. My partner and I had traveled in Europe and thought we would take an opportunity to return if one arose. When my original plans changed, I looked beyond the United States.

What was difficult about moving internationally?

The university system, graduate training, immigration paperwork, and everyday administration were all different. Even receiving mail in a language I could not read created small challenges.

It was not an overwhelming cultural divide, but there was still a lot to learn. Language tools and an increasingly international environment have made some of those barriers easier over time.

How do you make room for family?

For me, the issue is often family–work balance, because I enjoy science so much that it is part of what I want to do with my life.

But enjoying work does not remove obligations to a partner and children. Some time has to be protected. That can mean preserving weekends, turning down nonessential travel, or changing a meeting when family needs help.

You have to be respectful of the people sharing your life. It is possible, but it requires choices.

What skills do you think future researchers will need?

Curiosity, creativity, and the ability to communicate across disciplines remain essential. I also see data science becoming increasingly important: producing good data, exploring complex datasets, and finding patterns that people can interpret.

Collaboration is part of that future. Shared data and better communication make it possible for groups with different expertise to work together quickly. The tools change, but the ability to ask a good question and work with other people remains central.

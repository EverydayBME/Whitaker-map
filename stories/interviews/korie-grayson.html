Thank you for accepting our invitation for an interview, Dr. Grayson. If we’re all set to begin, my first question is- what brought you to the field of biomedical engineering?

What actually brought me to BME, or biomedical engineering, was the fact that I worked at a biomedical device company before I even applied to grad school. At the medical device company, I worked on a dialysis graft, called the HERO Graft. I worked on the manufacturing side where we made the graft from scratch daily. It had a venous component, an arterial component, and a lot of silicone coating in between. It was our jobs to make it efficiently with little to no defects and following certain standards and deadlines. Basically, our job was to make a quality product for when it entered the patient.

Within doing that, I realized I wanted to do more of the research and development side, or R&D. We would have this one person from R&D that would come in to see different ways to improve the grafts, either by changing the material of the graft or different components. I was just like, "Oh, I really want to do that job." In order to do that, you had to have a higher degree. This person had a master's, so I was like, "Okay, let me consider doing a grad program."

Because I worked right off of Georgia Tech's campus, I ended up reading and running into other biomedical engineers and a chemical engineer, who are now my friends and my mentors who encouraged me to apply to grad school. I realized, "Okay, I know I want to do biomedical engineering. I'm already kind of in that field, so let me just go ahead and apply." So I applied to grad school. I only applied to three schools. Two of them said, “No,” but Cornell said yes. I quit my job and I went straight into graduate school at Cornell for biomedical engineering.

I have never had an exact set plan. I didn't know that I was going to do biomedical engineering in undergrad. I didn't know that after undergrad. I only knew that when I actually went into an industry job, paused, and saw how biomedical devices could work. Then, I observed how biomedical engineering comes into play, and how you can make it so translational that it might actually end up in patients. I was like, "Yeah, this is what I want to do my degree in.”

How was your transition into graduate school?

It was a little bit of an adjustment at first because I was a chemistry “pre-med” undergrad major, but I ended up having to take a few undergrad courses to get a more solid engineering background. I didn't take differential equations, I literally did that in grad school, but at least I had a strong calculus background, so it wasn't too much of a jump. I found myself taking fluid mechanics, statistics, drug delivery; taking all these different courses in order to understand more about the engineering principles that can go into the medical field. That's kind of how I made my transition into a BME.

I think that's quite fascinating that your work as a biomedical engineer informed your decision to go into this field. You mentioned a little bit about peer mentors. Who else influenced you along this pathway and what role did your mentors play in your decision-making?

Actually, a lot! Even from high school, I remember my guidance counselor making me take AP calculus, making me take AP chem, and making me apply to schools and all these scholarships before I went into college. Then, when I got to college, my mentors and professors made sure I did research during the summer and made sure I kept up with my grades. You had to have a 3.0 GPA or higher for our scholarship, or you got kicked out, and I wasn't trying to pay so I worked hard. Also, having us connect with different people, I even met one of my mentors at a carwash. He was an OB GYN doctor that I shadowed for a couple of years, and he was the one that wrote a letter of recommendation for me, not only for my MD PhD programs when I applied, but also for grad school. I still talk to him to this day.

This encouragement of people willing to work with me and understand me really just pushed me in the right direction. When I was interested in grad school, my two friends and mentors said, "This is something that you can do." And then my other mentor said, "Okay, apply to NSF, make sure you have this." And even my mom would read over my essays. She may not have exactly understood exactly what I was writing about, but she was there to also push me and guide me and give me advice, as well. She's like the comma police, so she would definitely be all over my essays editing them. Again, she might not have understood all the concepts, but she was a helpful part as well.

Mentors definitely helped me along my path. I wouldn't even be here if it wasn't for certain people along the way from high school until now that pushed me into these different directions. Even when searching for a postdoc position, my PhD mentor and advisor was like, "Hey, I have a friend who is looking for a postdoc, talk to her." Then, the next thing you know, I'm here! My initial career path was leading more towards science policy, but I knew I still liked research. My current mentor and advisor is pushing me towards academia. It’s always mentors. They want to look out for the best for you, but they also see potential that sometimes you're not able to see or quite grasp, just yet.

That is such good insight. Our audience is largely young trainees, undergraduates, and even some high school students interested in engineering and biology and not really sure what to do. We think that it’s so helpful to hear how you met a doctor at a carwash and, just by keeping your ears and eyes open, you recognized someone who was excited about you and your trajectory. So, you started in medical devices where the hemodialysis work may involve fluid mechanics and kidney physiology. What did you focus on in your graduate program? Full disclaimer, I watch a little bit of your excellent thesis defense, but I hope we can start from the beginning for the interview.

Of course- In the beginning, I knew my research was going to be focused within the drug delivery field because that is what my advisor did. I wasn't exactly sure which disease mechanism, or which disease at all, I was going to focus on, but I knew we were definitely focusing on microfluidic devices, fluid flow, as well as types of mechanics, inflammation, and the inflammatory cascade.

When I joined, we had just released a paper talking about TRAIL and TRAIL liposomes. TRAIL is a protein that specifically targets cancer cells, and so we put that on liposomes, put those in flow with circulating tumor cells, and saw that they kill circulating tumor cells quite efficiently. Of course, this effect involved the properties of the inflammatory cascade and also fluid mechanics. Once we saw that, we asked, "What are some other ways we can do this? Can we apply this to different types of cancer?" I gravitated towards prostate cancer. I think, at the time, I found out that my grandfather had prostate cancer, and he died, unfortunately, two years ago from prostate cancer. That became my niche and what I wanted to stick with.

African American men, unfortunately, are disproportionately affected by prostate cancer versus any other race, and so that was the passion that hooked me onto a project in that field. I feel like it is very important that when you are doing your research you find something that you are passionate about. If not, those days when experiments aren't working, things aren't going right, you're frustrated even with the direction of the project- if you don't have passion behind it, you're most likely going to quit. That was what kept me going.

Initially, we were using an animal model for prostate cancer that we had previously validated. But again, we were just targeting circulating tumor cells within the bloodstream with the TRAIL liposomes. I wondered, "What's happening in the primary tumor?" You want to mitigate the effects and slow the growth of the primary tumor. You don't want it to keep growing and growing. My first project was looking into if these TRAIL liposomes were actually infiltrating the primary tumor. If so, what immune cells were they attaching to? E-selectin is a molecule expressed on inflammatory cells used to actually bind to leukocytes in the blood. If they are infiltrating, then who are they attaching to? And so within that work, currently still under review, we found that macrophages were the most abundant cell type within this NOD SCID animal model. They were actually latching onto these liposomes and infiltrating into the prostate tumor and causing, what we call, apoptosis or programmed cell death. And so that was exciting. Again, with research, you're never really done or feel like you're done. I feel like I could have done a lot more things with that project, but that was a good way of looking at our model and seeing why, with TRIAL liposomes, we were able to subdue the primary tumor growth, because they're actually infiltrating and they're doing it by attaching to these tumor-associated macrophages.

That led to us thinking, "What are some ways we can increase the efficacy of these TRAIL liposomes or these TRAIL-associated leukocytes?" I had the idea to use something already in clinic because I liked the translational aspect. What if we use or look at the two chemotherapeutics that are used for late-stage prostate cancer and combine them with the TRAIL liposome therapy? When I did that, I found that it worked really well. The chemotherapy sensitized the cancer cells to TRAIL pretty efficiently.

In another paper that is currently under review I saw that there was significant apoptosis when you do the combination therapy. It was a synergistic effect. My advisor aked, "Well, what's the mechanism? What exactly is happening behind it?" The rest of my PhD was figuring out the mechanism and how that worked. We saw that ER stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, was one of the reasons. In theory, it's increasing the death receptor expression on the surface of the cells, which is what TRAIL binds to, and therefore increasing the potency of the TRAIL liposome therapy.

I did that work in a 2D platform, and then I also did it in a 3D platform. I actually made tumor spheroids and then studied the same type of combination therapy. I moved from 2D to 3D because it just made logical sense for me, that 3D is more physiologically relevant. Why can drugs fail at certain stages throughout the whole clinical trial process and getting to humans? It's partly because we don't have those translational models that are exact to what humans are. Yes, we have animal models and we do have 2D cell cultures, but 2D cells don't act the same way as those grown into 3D cell culture. And animals- we have similar, but not the same genetic makeup. So sometimes things can get lost in translation. Going from 2D to 3D was moving forward, figuring out the best way and using different engineering principles to grow 3D spheroids of consistent sizes and diameters,

Both of those projects are also in the current review process. That was basically my whole PhD.

A research question: did you have a chance to check for off-target effects on healthy cells? Were you unable to include healthy non-tumor cell lines in these models?

Unfortunately, we actually weren't able to do that directly. I stuck with using our gold standard prostate cancer cell lines. Even when moving into 3D, we would incorporate fibroblasts, which are known to make up the tumor microenvironment. In a way, the healthy cells were cancer-associated fibroblasts versus normal fibroblasts.

Even when we were doing our treatments, we noticed that there was still a small population of surviving cells. It brought to light the fact that there are cancer stem cells that are in your body that you also have to target and treat. We know they are responsible for replication and self-renewal, like normal stem cells, which is why they contribute to tumor spreading and growth.

After a while, my advisor was like, "Korie, you just got to graduate, so stop looking at stuff." But that's definitely something you have your control- your cells, if you want, but we usually stick with cancer cells. That's the kind of population we want to study. Then, we just incorporate different cells that make up the tumor microenvironment, cancer-associated versus healthy normal fibroblasts, and we do our comparisons from there.

Thank you. I especially love the beginning part about your grandfather as an inspiration. A special element in biomedical engineering and translational research is that you can actually imagine a recipient who could benefit from what you do, versus a purely technical question. This imparts a sense of urgency and a sense of personal connection in our research.

I'll just ask one more question. You mentioned that you don't know where your career trajectory is going. You're in Michigan right now. What is most exciting about where you imagine yourself in the next few years?

I think the most exciting part for me is being able to venture into a new research area. I got my PhD in biomedical engineering, and now I'm doing a postdoc in chemical engineering. See how the world has come back around? Chemistry and BME, chemical engineering, right? So it’s not to say I'm unfamiliar – obviously I have a chemistry background – but there are some things that I didn't necessarily have to think about during my PhD that now I have to do a little bit more research about. My excitement is more about being in a new field and actually learning, even though I have a PhD and I have my own field and project that I might be an expert in. Now, I'm somewhere new and I have to learn from the graduate students in the lab.

I offer expertise in certain areas like, "Maybe we should try this technique or maybe try that assay." Or, "You want to do this against a standard curve." Or I teach them about my research or my work as it related to theirs, but there still are so many things that I'm learning. That's what a postdoc is. You move into a new area, you learn new things, and the learning curve is different. Thankfully I have a wonderful advisor and a good group of grad students that are hardworking, willing to learn, open to changing, etc., and I just bring more of my wisdom of going through a PhD program. When they're freaking out about something, I can tell them, "Calm down. You're not going to get it right the first time." Or, "Let's just sit down and let's think about it." Or, "You can use this, you can do that."

I'm still not even the expert in what they're talking about, but I think I can offer that part of their experience. I know what it's like to be a grad student and I can help them work through their experiment planning and problem solving in just more of a relaxed way, because when you actually have your PhD, it does get a lot less hectic. You're not running around constantly. You're a little bit more reserved. You know how to think through a problem. You know it takes time, you know it takes energy, you know you may not get stuff right on the first time versus in grad school, where you had these unrealistic expectations that you would. I also think I can bestow onto them just a little bit more of a relaxing, calming feeling and letting them know everything's going to be okay, but still learning from them, about their research, how I can help, and different techniques that I can master.

Again, it's just a new time of knowledge and growth. With a PhD, you're never done, and even then, your final work is never the final say so in the field. It's always constantly evolving. And so just to be a part and having that experience of going through that and being able to help other people, mentor other students, is more of the exciting part for me. I like watching them grow. My advisors say, "You know, you'd make a really good PI." And I'm like, "I don't know how to do that, yet. I don't know.”

I think as far as career trajectory, there are still so many other things that I am very passionate about. I'm very passionate about self-advocacy, especially for women in science, DEI work, having influence and showing people that "This is what a scientist looks like." That is why I cannot necessarily be like, "Oh yeah. I'm to be an associate professor, tenure track." Or, "I'm going into industry." Or, “I might do science policy.” There are so many different things you can do with a PhD, which is so lovely. If you're interested in doing anything you dream, then you should get a PhD because you will have gone through that process and you will feel like you can do anything after that. That's how I feel. My options are open. My career options are open to where I could do and be anything I wanted to be.

That gave me goosebumps. That's exactly what I need to hear right now, as I'm facing an upcoming proposal defense in three or four months. Especially the idea of a PhD teaching you what hard work looks like. You've got to chip away at it while being calm and collected so that you can wake up and do it again the next day. Its about knowing what that feels like. That’s really helpful to hear, thanks.

I always say it's the biggest self-discovery journey you can ever go on. You learn so much about yourself, about your strengths and your weakness, your patience, your impatience, your ability to believe in yourself, your motivating factors, and your passions. You learn so much in a PhD program that, even though the final product is a dissertation, your final product should be who you are as a person and as a scientist. And if you haven't changed from year one to year six, hopefully, or by seven, eight, or nine, then there's something that didn't happen right in that whole process. I think people feel like, “I have to turn out data, I have to turn out a publication, I have to do this.” That's just one of the goals, but it's also about the impact that it has on yourself and your impact on others. In your PhD program, are you also volunteering? Are you also reaching out? It can be so solitary that we just focus on ourselves, which is good for a certain point, but our work and our research in science should impact the world and impact people. I feel like that's what should also be happening during a PhD program. So I say, “Take that leadership course, go ahead and do that extra activity. If you have a hobby, indulge into it.” That just makes you a well-rounded person, a well-rounded researcher, and a better citizen in society, because we need those right now. We definitely need them.

And then, when you reach the end, I promise, you're going to feel so much better. When you hear, "Dr. Sikorski." It's just going to be the biggest relief of your life. And then take it in, take a week, two weeks, off, whatever you got to do. Just indulgent and rub on the fact like, "Okay, I'm done. And I made it."

Thank you so much. Dr. Korie Grayson, PhD, is a Postdoctoral Researcher at the University of Michigan.

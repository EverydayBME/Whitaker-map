Jeff Hsiao

Finding a research home in Zurich

ETH Zurich

What are you working on at ETH Zurich?

I am a research assistant in Andrew deMello’s group. Much of the group’s work involves microfluidics: controlling fluids at very small scales, where their behavior can be useful for different applications.

My own project is more fundamental. I study how enzymes interact with nanoparticle bioconjugates. I am not currently working on a microfluidic platform, although that is a longer-term possibility for the project.

The technology includes spherical nucleic acids, which you can picture as tiny particles with DNA attached around them. Such systems can be designed to produce a signal in response to something inside a cell. That makes them interesting for sensing and diagnostics, although my work is focused on understanding the underlying interactions.

How did you choose Zurich for your Whitaker fellowship?

An undergraduate professor knew my interests and pointed me toward the group. He knew about the Whitaker program and helped me see an opportunity that I might not have found on my own.

I looked into the research and the city. Zurich seemed like somewhere I could do interesting science and also enjoy life outside work. Both mattered in deciding where to go.

It was my first research experience abroad and my first time in Europe. I really jumped in.

What was that transition like?

I was fortunate: I found an apartment fairly easily, and the laboratory was welcoming. The group is very international, and we work in English, which helped me feel comfortable.

There were practical adjustments. The cost of everyday things, especially eating out, surprised me. Even if you have lived in an expensive American city, you still have to learn what daily life costs somewhere new.

Overall, though, the transition was smooth. I have found Zurich a comfortable place to live.

What do you gain from being in an international laboratory?

People bring different educational experiences, skills, and ways of approaching a problem. I would not automatically attribute every difference to nationality, but the variety within the group is useful.

Someone may suggest a method you would not have considered because they have worked in another area. Having people around who can ask, “Why not try this?” has helped me scientifically.

What makes the research feel worthwhile to you?

Over time, personal experiences can change how you see your work. My father is blind in one eye. I would not say that was the original reason I chose science, but as I learned more, it helped me appreciate why work related to health matters.

I am also interested in technologies that could make care more accessible. Many people cannot easily reach the kinds of facilities or resources that others take for granted. Developing something useful beyond well-resourced settings is an idea that motivates me.

How do you make time to step away from research?

I have to make it a priority. There will always be something else to finish, so rest needs a place in the schedule too.

Being somewhere new helps because there are places I want to explore. I enjoy hiking and photography, which go well together: I can go for a walk, stop to take pictures, and do something with a different rhythm from the laboratory.

A break does not always need to be a major holiday. Sometimes it is simply getting outside for fresh air.

How important has mentoring been?

It has been essential. A mentor can help with something immediate, like choosing a class, or with a much bigger question about your career.

My professor’s guidance is part of the reason I ended up here. He understood what interested me and suggested a direction. I am very grateful for that.

What advice would you give someone early in their studies?

Keep asking questions—of other people and of yourself. Ask professors, mentors, and people you trust. Try to find what makes you curious, and give yourself room to explore before committing to one direction.

It is also worth asking why you want to do the work. You do not need every answer immediately, but paying attention to those questions can help you choose a path that fits you.

Keep exploring

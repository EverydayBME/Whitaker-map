Angus Johnston

Monash University

2019 · 2 minute read

What does your day-to-day look like?

During teaching semesters one day a week is taken up with teaching. I teach in the pharmaceutical sciences, so I teach some of the molecular biology and also the nanotechnology side of things. Probably like most people in the area, our passion is the research. Teaching students is good. Admin of teaching is not good. So the direct interaction with students is always good, but all that paperwork is... well, as you can see it's piling up on my desk.

On the research side of things, we are trying to understand the interactions of nanoengineered materials with cells and to apply a combination of physical chemistry techniques, which is what my PhD was originally on. Then, we incorporate more modern molecular biology techniques to understand trafficking pathways and also to control them to achieve better therapeutic outcomes.

So my group performs a lot of microscopy. I don't spend as much time in the lab as I would like, but I get at least half a day per week, sometimes a day per week, to do mostly microscopy in the lab. The rest of my group does a lot of protein engineering, protein expression. I don't do any of the hands-on stuff for that, but I still keep my hand in the microscopy.

Could you tell me a little bit more about the projects that are in your portfolio right now?

Our overarching interest is trying to understand how we can target drugs specifically to cells, and then, once they reach those cells, how they are trafficked inside the cells. At the targeting level, one of the things that we're interested in is engineering proteins, so we can control their orientation on the surface of the particle. If we can control the orientation of proteins, obviously their 3D structure plays an important role in their function. If you just have random orientation of proteins on the surface, then the majority of the protein won't be active. So we've been using a technique called stop codon reassignment, so we can take naturally occurring proteins or engineered proteins and change the naturally occurring stop codon to code for a completely new amino acid. And that new amino acid allows us to attach the proteins onto particles in a particular orientation.

Tell me about your undergraduate experience and how you got into engineering.

I had no idea what I wanted to do when I got out of high school. I thought I might want to be a doctor; I probably had good enough grades to go to other schools in Canada or in the US even if I had applied. But I also had something very new at the end of high school, which I had never had before: a girlfriend. So, I decided to stay in Calgary where I grew up. But I started out in pre-med at University of Calgary and I did fine, but I really didn't find it very exciting. Meanwhile, a bunch of my friends from high school had gone into engineering. And at that point in my life I really didn't know, or care about, what engineering was. I had never really strongly considered it as a career. I would sit in on their lectures; one of those classes was called Computing For Engineers. I said to myself, “Oh, this seems interesting!” I had loved computers. I had loved playing video games. I had never coded before though. But I started going to all their lectures and I eventually ended up going to the whole course just as like an unofficial auditor.

A lot of Canadian schools are co-op based. You come and go and do 4 to 6-month work terms interspersed with going back to class. U of Calgary has an excellent internship program but has a different approach. Before your senior year, everyone has the option (and is encouraged) to take a full year to go work in industry or conduct research in a lab; the idea was to make some money, create a strong professional network, and get some technical experience before you finished your degree and graduated. At Calgary, most students would get an internship at oil and gas or energy sector company. I got wind early on in my junior year, however, that there were these very competitive programs where you could go to Switzerland or Japan for a year. You had to have good GPA as well as write a bunch of essays. For me, Japan sounded like a cool experience. I had never lived away from Calgary before and I had never traveled. I had been to Europe a couple of times, but I had never traveled to a place that was as different as Japan from Calgary. Lo and behold, I applied for the co-op Japan program got in.

My job was in the broadcast multimedia department of Sanyo Electric. This was before the heyday of streaming services that we now live in 2002 or 2003. The idea that you could broadcast streaming video from a server to multiple clients simultaneously was kind of the frontier, as far as I could tell. And Sanyo had a lot of irons in the fire and wanted to basically write code to do that. And so, this was my task. However, this was complicated by a particularly different aspect of corporate culture in Japan: they like to move people around. The rationale is that you can sort of enrich your peers that way and your own professional experience. Even if you're doing really, really well at your job after five years in a department, they'll move you to a completely different area. When I showed up on day one, management essentially told me, “So, we had this guy who wrote a bunch of code and none of us understand it. Could you figure it out?”

Looking back, my co-op experience continues to be that experience that changed everything for me, in terms of exposing me to a completely different culture. You know, I was living in my parents' house when I was going to school in Calgary; I wasn't living in a dorm and so I'd never lived on my own. It was also just enormously interesting to meet other people. Both Japanese people and other foreigners living there.

Tell me about how you got into BME.

After my internship in Japan, I didn't have anything lined up for afterwards—in part because I didn’t envision myself living and working in Japan long-term. And so, because of that, about halfway through my senior year, I had been sort of nosing around I found out that, “Oh, you can get paid to go to grad school.” This was news to me. Like I had no idea. I had been basically going to classes and working part time to help put myself through school. The notion that you could get paid to go to school was completely foreign to me. I had no idea that you could get a stipend. Moreover, someone explained to me that there are these federal funding programs. Just like how in the US, you could get an F31; in Canada, you can apply to NSERC, the National Science and Engineering Research Council, for an award to supplement the cost of your master's. Furthermore, because Alberta was doing extremely well at the time, the Alberta government was topping up people's stipends if they got a grant from the NSERC. And so, what it amounted to was that I could get paid like ~30K to do my masters, which was not so far off from what a some of my classmates were getting as entry level salaries at engineering companies.

We had a signal processing class, a biomedical signal analysis. So, I had learned about ECG and EEG and EMG, that kind of stuff. But I really didn't have any foundations of like what would resemble a BME degree. And that wasn't offered in Calgary at the time. Even when I was a grad student, we didn't have any biomedical engineering degree program. There was a specialization came along I think shortly after I graduated where you could take like an extra semester worth of classes and get an electrical engineering degree, for example, with a specialization in biomedical engineering. But even still to this day, they don't give out bachelor’s degrees in BME. That’s actually the case for many Canadian universities. But they did have a graduate program. And so, I realized I really liked the research, which happened very quickly as I got sort of consumed by learning about arrhythmia and learning about finite element modeling, simulations, and all this stuff that I now do. Naturally, I decided to pursue a PhD and so I applied to transfer from the electrical engineering master's program into the BME PhD program. Got accepted to that, got some more funding from the government. And then just continued to do research.

It took a long time for me to find my footing. You know, like I said, I had never done research as an undergrad. It really didn't click for me until about a year or two in. I think I was a really immature undergrad. I was really good at taking tests, but I didn't consider myself a good student. I think I did become a good student in grad school because I realized how interested I was in actually knowing the stuff, in actually understanding and really thinking critically about how to solve these problems.

On BME in Canada and trying to get an engineering faculty position

BME doesn't exist in the same way as it does in the US and increasingly in the rest of the world. Only a handful of departments in the entire country confer BME degrees; the rest are these sorts of specializations and hybrid programs. It's taken a lot longer for it to become established. And so, when I was on the job hunt and I would interview with a medical school, they would way things like, “You know, the long and the short of it is this: It would be weird for us to hire an engineer. We're a little bit worried that if you come here and submitted grants to the Canadian Institute for Health Research (the NIH equivalent in the US), they're going to think you're too much of an engineer and they're not going to fund you.” Then I would go interview with engineering departments and I would get the opposite answer. “Well, we like your stuff, we think you do really good work. But it's a little too medically.” There seems to be an aversion in the Canadian funding system to you know NSERC really doesn't want to get into a situation where a bunch of medical research is finding its way to them instead of to the CIHR. I find that pretty frustrating and to some extent I think it’s counterproductive and it's stifling innovation in the country where I grew up, which in many ways I still consider home. I don’t want to say that’s the only reason I didn’t have any luck finding a job at a Canadian school, some of that falls on me. But I do certainly think it was a factor.

When did you realize you wanted to pursue a career in academia?

To be honest, I was never at 100% until the day I signed the offer with UW. I was close – 95% or higher, but the thought always lingered in the back of my mind: “What if I just got a quote-unquote Real Job?” Funny, as I'm a professor now and I was a senior researcher then, and I know in my head that those things are very much Real Jobs! But I still don't fully think of it as a job. I think of myself as a lifelong student, and I relate to my students in that way, whether they work with me in the lab or I teach them in the classroom. I've never lost that mindset that when I get up in the morning, I'm going to school.

In any case, during those years, I would talk to family members and people who I respected a lot and they would say “Look, you’re in your 30s, these are your peak earning years. You need to get going!” And when I took stock of the scenario, I realized – yes, this is true I'm not socking a lot of money away as a postdoc. But there's a flip side to that too, which is that, being in a research position confers a certain amount of stability it's truly a wonderful job. Nevertheless, I freely there was always doubt in my mind until the day I signed that contract! Just like being a postdoc, being a professor is a wonderful job too, but when it's hard, it's incredibly hard. But then again, my suspicion is that's true of every job. Everybody who I know who's gainfully employed has really, really difficult parts of their job. So, maybe there's no good answer to your question.

On teaching.

I'm prepping for my course this spring right now, and it's a heck of a lot of work. And if I'm not careful, it'll completely take over. But man, like nothing makes me happier than knowing that I get to play a role in the lives of these 70 students per year who are some of the brightest students in our state. And I get to help shape their view of the world and I get to help them create their careers. It’s really exhilarating.

Thoughts about the state of BME education.

We continue to have a bit of an identity crisis. Who are we in? What do we do? There's always been this provocative sort of issue. Traditional engineering disciplines, like civil, electrical, mechanical, and chemical, all have clear core competencies. We don't have that really yet in BioE. Different institutions will have extraordinarily different curriculums. There are bioengineering programs with a strong focus on biotransport, others that are biomaterials oriented, or data science oriented, or systems oriented. It's really kind of a mixed bag. And I think that's arisen from just the fact that the professors who teach BME at different universities are so different from each other. It's such a heterogeneous mix.

I’ve been thinking about this a lot lately and I feel like BME has come in waves. We’ve had the era of biomedical engineers being device-focused and technology-focused. This was wave one. They’ve established themselves and they continue to do great things. Wave two was recognizing that bench lab experiments, grounded in traditional biology, physiology, chemistry, and so on – these can all be augmented greatly by incorporating engineering design principles. For me, the third wave is happening right now, and it involves integration of bioengineers alongside clinicians, in some cases right at the bedside in the hospital. More and more, we're going to see engineers who are working in those milieus as opposed to just at or near the bench. My suspicion is that structure will need to be driven by programs like ours at universities. From what I’ve absorbed, talking to my former classmates who have moved on to work in the biotech sector, it seems like today’s companies are not as well-suited as universities are to execute that kind of vision.

What would you tell a younger version of yourself and what advice would you give, or what advice did you wish you had?

When you graduate from your PhD, a year is going to feel like a very long time. When you finish your postdoc and start your lab, if you're on the academic career path, a year is not going to feel like a very long time, especially if kids ever enter the equation. Like if you have kids, all of a sudden time ends up compressing and what used to feel like an eternity starts to feel like it's just flying by. So, be cognizant of the fact that it's becoming increasingly common for people doing postdocs to spend three, four years in that type of position. And I really wish we had gone into our Baltimore period more clear-eyed about that. Because we would have made very different decisions. We would have tried harder to buy a house and we would have taken a much different approach to creating a pathway towards US citizenship for ourselves. Those are both really challenging systems to navigate.

More generally, I guess the theme of my advice would be this: be careful what you assume the future holds. Because it's often times going to prove very different from what you expect. And I think the with regards to academic career planning, no matter how it looks from your vantage point now, nothing can really prepare you for how challenging and how complex it is to really be on your own as a PI.

On the challenges of being a professor.

I used to be one of these people who was just like, I can't believe how rude this professor is, taking so long to answer a really simple email. And yeah... I'm that guy now. I'm the guy not answering those emails and it's because I’m completely overwhelmed so, so many things. You know, it really is like running your own little tiny company within a very large machine. You’ve got to hustle, you've got to be organized, you have to learn to delegate work, which I've never been great at. And I’m learning, but it’s so very hard to learn!
